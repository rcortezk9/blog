Summary

St. John Chrysostom (c. 347–407 AD) is one of the Three Holy Hierarchs and Ecumenical Teachers of the Orthodox Church, and the most prolific homilist in the patristic tradition. His name Chrysostomos — "Golden-Mouthed" — was a posthumous epithet earned by his preaching. He is the author of the Divine Liturgy bearing his name, which is the standard Sunday and weekday Eucharistic liturgy of the Orthodox Church. He represents the Antiochene school of biblical exegesis: historically grounded, typologically disciplined, never purely allegorical.

Key Points

  • Archbishop of Constantinople (398–404): The most prominent episcopal see in the East; his tenure was cut short by court intrigue and two exiles
  • Author of the Divine Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom: The standard Orthodox Eucharistic liturgy, derived from the Antiochene liturgical tradition; served on most Sundays and weekdays
  • Antiochene exegete: Reads Scripture historically-literally first, then typologically — rooted in the actual events; contrasts with the Alexandrian allegorism of Origen; trained under Diodore of Tarsus
  • Homiletical corpus: Among the largest of any Church Father — 90 homilies on Matthew, 88 on John, 55 on Acts, 44 on Romans, 32 on 1 Corinthians (including Homily 26 on head coverings and the Eucharist), homilies on all the Pauline epistles and Hebrews
  • Social theology: His preaching on wealth and poverty is among the most radical in Christian history — "Not to share your own wealth with the poor is theft from the poor and deprivation of their means of life"
  • Feast days: November 13 (repose); January 27 (transfer of relics to Constantinople, 438); January 30 (The Three Hierarchs — with Basil the Great and Gregory the Theologian)
  • Exile and death: Deposed twice through Empress Eudoxia's court maneuvering; died 407 in Comana, Pontus during a forced march of exile; last words: "Glory to God for all things"

Details

Life and Context

Born in Antioch (~347 AD) to a Christian mother, Anthusa. Educated by the pagan rhetor Libanius — by one account, when asked who his best student was, Libanius said "John, had the Christians not stolen him." Studied Scripture and theology under Diodore of Tarsus, the leading figure of the Antiochene school. Baptized (~368), spent several years as an ascetic in the Syrian desert before illness forced his return to Antioch. Ordained deacon (381) and priest (386) in Antioch, where he spent 12 years preaching — the period that produced most of his homiletical corpus.

Appointed Archbishop of Constantinople in 398. His tenure was reformist and confrontational: he sold off luxury furnishings of the episcopal palace, reduced clerical spending, confronted corrupt clergy, and preached directly against the wealthy elite — including the Empress Eudoxia. The Synod of the Oak (403) was a kangaroo court engineered by Bishop Theophilus of Alexandria and Empress Eudoxia that deposed him on fabricated charges. Emperor Arcadius exiled him but recalled him within days after an earthquake the empress interpreted as divine judgment. His second exile (404) came when he again preached against the erection of a silver statue of Eudoxia near Hagia Sophia. He died in exile (407) on a forced march under deliberately harsh conditions.

His relics were returned to Constantinople in 438 under Emperor Theodosius II. The Three Hierarchs feast (January 30) was established in the 11th century to resolve a dispute over which of the three — Basil, Gregory, or Chrysostom — was the greatest.

The Divine Liturgy

The Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom is the standard Eucharistic liturgy of the Orthodox Church, served on most Sundays and weekdays. The Liturgy of St. Basil the Great (longer) is served ten times annually. Chrysostom's liturgy is a revision and compression of the Antiochene liturgical tradition; while the exact extent of his personal composition is debated, the tradition of the Church attributes the current form to his editorship.

The Anaphora (Eucharistic prayer) of his Liturgy is the primary Orthodox expression of anamnesis:

"Remembering therefore this saving commandment and all those things which have come to pass for us — the Cross, the Tomb, the Resurrection on the third day, the Ascension into heaven, the Sitting at the right hand, and the second and glorious Coming…"

This is the liturgical enactment of 1 Corinthians 11:24–25 — not mere recollection but the making-present of the one sacrifice of Christ. The Liturgy also contains the Eucharistic warning Ta hagia tois hagiois ("Holy Things for the Holy") — the congregation's self-examination built into the rite itself, grounding the discipline of v. 28.

His Preparatory Prayers are included in the Canon of Preparation read before Holy Communion, still used throughout the Orthodox Church today.

Homily 26 on 1 Corinthians

His commentary on 1 Corinthians 11 (Homily 26) is the primary patristic interpretation of both the head coverings and Eucharistic sections:

On head coverings: Chrysostom rejects the subordination-only reading and presses the glory theology — human glory must be veiled before divine glory in worship. He reads the woman's veil as analogous to the iconostasis: the veil hides human glory so that divine glory can be approached. The covering is a liturgical act of reverence, not a social marker of status.

On the Eucharist: He is fierce on the rich/poor division at Corinth. He applies the "discerning the body" (v. 29) rebuke directly to wealthy Antiochenes in his own congregation: "You dishonor this table when you do not judge him worthy of sharing your food, and yet you say he is worthy to share in this Body… Are you honoring the table, or disgracing it?" For Chrysostom, failure to see Christ in the poor and failure to recognize Christ in the Eucharist are not alternative readings — they are two expressions of the same blindness.

Antiochene Exegetical Method

Chrysostom represents the Antiochene school in contrast to the Alexandrian. The key distinctions:

AlexandrianAntiochene
Allegorical reading primaryHistorical-literal reading primary
Origen as the towering figureDiodore of Tarsus, Theodore of Mopsuestia
Spiritual sense may detach from historical meaningTypology must be rooted in the literal-historical event
Philo's influence visibleStoic rhetoric influences the homiletical style

Chrysostom's homilies are therefore dense with attention to the Greek text, historical context, and the moral/practical application — rather than abstract spiritual meanings. His preaching is theoria in the hermeneutical sense: seeing through the text to its full spiritual significance without abandoning its historical ground.

Social Theology

Chrysostom's preaching on wealth is consistently the most challenging material in his homiletical corpus:

  • "Not to share your own wealth with the poor is theft from the poor and deprivation of their means of life; we do not possess our own wealth but theirs."
  • He held that private accumulation was itself a form of injustice — the wealthy hold in common what belongs to all
  • His attacks on Eudoxia's luxury were not personal but consistent with a pattern: he sold the episcopal palace furnishings and used the funds for hospitals and care for the sick

This social edge is inseparable from his Eucharistic theology: you cannot receive Christ's Body and then step over Christ's Body in the poor. The "discerning the body" passage (1 Cor 11:29) is the scriptural hinge on which both turn.
