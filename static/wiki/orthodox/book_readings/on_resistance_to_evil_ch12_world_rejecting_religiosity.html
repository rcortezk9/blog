#asceticism#book/on_resistance_to_evil_by_force#book_study#moral_theology#orthodox#theology

"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son..."
— John 3:16

Before you read: This chapter goes for the theological jugular. Ilyin argues that Tolstoy's non-resistance rests on a genuinely incoherent religious picture of the world — sometimes an idyllic divine order where force is simply unnecessary, sometimes a fallen realm of sin from which one must flee — and that both pictures are invoked selectively, whichever supports passivity in the moment. The chapter's most severe passage exposes what a consistent version of this theology would actually mean God wills: that villains be given "unquestionably and undeniably" free rein over the weak. Read it as a test of whether "leaving it to God's will" is genuine piety or a cover for something else.

Chapter Overview

Ilyin argues that Tolstoy's world-rejection is not, at bottom, a moral conclusion but a religious one — and an internally contradictory one. Tolstoy holds two incompatible views of the external world simultaneously: first, an idyllic vision in which nature is divine, harmonious, and law-governed by God's own will, so that violence is simply unnecessary because the world already runs on love; second, a vision in which the world is a realm of sin, struggle, and competing instinct, so thoroughly fallen that participation in it (economic striving, sexual love, political power) is itself corrupting, and the only faithful response is radical simplification, asceticism, even the rejection of marriage and procreation. Both views, despite contradicting each other, converge on the same practical conclusion: withdrawal. Ilyin then dissects Tolstoy's own illustration — a bystander facing a robber's knife raised over a victim, who reasons that since he "cannot know" whether intervention will do more good than harm, he should not intervene — and shows this "epistemic humility" is really a cover for abdication, since it counsels certainty is required for restraint (never acting) but not for resistance (acting). The chapter's severest argument follows: if the external world's evil is nonetheless somehow within God's providential will, and if resisting that evil is what Tolstoy calls "usurping God's right," then the logical conclusion is monstrous — God wills that villains be given free, unhindered access to their victims, and anyone who takes up the sword to stop them is "declared an immoral and irreligious man," while the villain's "ferocity remains inviolably sacred." Ilyin closes by naming what has actually happened: Tolstoy's "religion" is not a connection between man and God at all, but a sentimental connection between man and man, mediated entirely by pity for suffering rather than by any genuine encounter with the divine — starting from man rather than from God, and therefore incapable of the "strong-willed, heroic struggle" true religion wages against evil in the world it has accepted as God's own burden to bear.

Main Points

1. Tolstoy Holds Two Contradictory Religious Pictures of the World, Both Ending in Withdrawal

Core Argument: Tolstoy's rejection of the external world rests on two mutually incompatible religious visions — nature as a divine, harmonious order in which force is unnecessary, and nature as an irredeemably fallen realm of struggle from which one must flee — and both, despite their contradiction, are deployed selectively to justify the same conclusion: withdrawal from active engagement with the world.

Historical Context: Ilyin is exposing an internal incoherence at the theological foundation of non-resistance that the earlier chapters' moral and psychological critiques had not yet reached — the problem is not only Tolstoy's ethics but the unstable religious cosmology beneath them.

Biblical Foundation: John 3:16's declaration that "God so loved the world" that He sent His Son establishes a single, coherent Christian stance toward creation — fallen, yet loved and worth entering into and redeeming — that refuses both of Tolstoy's extremes: neither an already-perfect idyll requiring no struggle, nor an irredeemable realm to be abandoned.

Patristic Witness: The Orthodox doctrine of creation as fundamentally good but fallen and in need of redemption (rather than either wholly divine as-is, or wholly corrupt and to be fled) reflects the coherent middle Ilyin says Tolstoy's oscillation lacks — the Fathers neither idealize the fallen world nor counsel total withdrawal from it, but call for its transfiguration through engaged, costly struggle.

Sub-Points:

  • A. In the idyllic view, "the world is driven by love," even animals "live in it peacefully," and labor and childbearing are blessed callings rather than compromises.
  • B. In the fallen view, the world is "a world of sin, sadness, and strife," governed by "the law of struggle for existence," in which economic competition, sexual love, and political power are all condemned as forms of violence.
  • C. Both views converge on asceticism, radical simplification, and non-resistance — Ilyin notes they are "connected by one common thread: the rejection of violence," regardless of which cosmology is invoked to justify it.

Practical Application: When a position shifts between two contradictory justifications depending on which one is needed in the moment, this instability itself is evidence that the underlying conclusion — not the stated reasons for it — is what is actually doing the work; a coherent Christian stance toward the world must hold together both its fallen state and its enduring belovedness by God.

Catechumenate Note: This equips a catechumen to recognize and resist the same instability in more modern forms — both an over-idealized "the world is basically fine, don't worry" spirituality and a despairing "the world is irredeemably corrupt, withdraw entirely" spirituality are equally departures from the Orthodox call to engage the world as God's own beloved, if fallen, creation.

2. Tolstoy's "Epistemic Humility" About Consequences Selectively Excuses Only Inaction

Core Argument: Tolstoy's argument that one cannot know whether intervention (killing a brigand to save his victim) will actually help — since the brigand might not have struck anyway — is applied only to justify inaction, never to question the safety of not intervening; genuine uncertainty about consequences cuts both ways, but Tolstoy's use of it systematically favors passivity.

Historical Context: Ilyin quotes Tolstoy's own illustration directly to expose this asymmetry — the appeal to unknowable consequences functions as a one-way rhetorical device rather than a genuinely neutral epistemic principle.

Biblical Foundation: The steward entrusted with talents in Matthew 25:14-30 is condemned specifically for the excuse of caution and inaction ("I was afraid, and went and hid thy talent") — Scripture treats uncertainty about outcomes as no excuse for failing to act when action is called for, precisely the asymmetry Ilyin identifies in Tolstoy's reasoning.

Patristic Witness: The Church's consistent teaching that moral responsibility attaches to right intention and reasonable judgment in the moment, not to certain foreknowledge of consequences (which no one possesses), stands against Tolstoy's selective invocation of uncertainty as grounds specifically for non-intervention.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Tolstoy's own words, as Ilyin quotes them: "I am not absolutely certain what the brigand will do. He might not strike, while I would surely kill him" — used to justify not acting.
  • B. Ilyin notes this reasoning never considers that the uncertainty could equally argue for acting to prevent a probable, serious harm rather than gambling on an uncertain restraint by the attacker.
  • C. This "gross superstition" (as Tolstoy himself calls the presumption that one can foresee and change the future) is applied inconsistently — it excuses inaction while never being turned to question whether inaction itself might be the more dangerous gamble.

Practical Application: Whenever an argument from uncertainty is used to justify only one course of action (typically inaction), it is worth testing whether the same uncertainty, honestly applied, would counsel the opposite conclusion just as plausibly.

Catechumenate Note: This is a useful discernment tool for any situation where "we can't know for sure" is invoked to avoid a difficult but seemingly necessary intervention — genuine humility about the future should not become a one-directional excuse for always choosing the path of least resistance.

3. A Consistent Non-Resistance Theology Requires God to Will Villains' Unhindered Access to Victims

Core Argument: If the world's evil is nonetheless within God's providential will, and resisting evil by force is "usurping God's right" as Tolstoy claims, then the only consistent conclusion is that God wills the villain's ferocity to remain "inviolably sacred" and unhindered while condemning as blasphemy anyone who intervenes to protect the weak — a conclusion Ilyin presents as the reductio ad absurdum exposing the whole framework's incoherence.

Historical Context: This is the chapter's rhetorical climax, following directly from the contradictions named in the first section — Ilyin is not exaggerating Tolstoy's position for effect but showing what it actually, logically entails if followed to its consistent end.

Biblical Foundation: Romans 13:4's description of the civil authority as "a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil" directly contradicts the notion that God wills evildoers' ferocity to go unhindered — Scripture explicitly sanctions and even commands resistance to evildoers through instituted authority, precisely the opposite of Tolstoy's implied theology.

Patristic Witness: The Church's veneration of warrior-saints and righteous rulers who defended the innocent by force (with due canonical acknowledgment of the moral weight involved, as in St. Basil's canons) stands as a direct, lived refutation of any theology that would treat resisting evil as usurping God's prerogative.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin's core exposure: "when the villain is allowed to assault the weak and corrupt the soul of a child, it means that such is pleasing to god, but when someone who is not a villain wants to thwart this villain, it does not please god."
  • B. He names the deep contradiction directly: "on the one hand, god is love and wants from people mutual compassion and unity, but on the other hand, he wants villainy with freedom and impunity for the villains."
  • C. Ilyin diagnoses the selective mechanism at work: "a sentimental moralist... accepts the will of god when this acceptance leads him to a passive enjoyment of pity, and does not accept it when this acceptance would lead him to a heroic volitional service."

Practical Application: Any theological appeal to "leaving it to God's will" should be tested for this same selectivity — does the appeal to providence consistently apply to all outcomes, or only to the outcome (inaction) already preferred on other grounds?

Catechumenate Note: This is an important safeguard against a passive misuse of trust in providence — genuine submission to God's will does not mean refusing the responsibilities and vocations (including, at times, forceful protection of the vulnerable) that God's providence has also placed within human hands to exercise.

4. True Religion Starts From God and Accepts the World's Burden; Tolstoy's Doctrine Starts From Man and Rejects It

Core Argument: Genuine religion begins with God and moves outward to accept the world — including its evil — as a burden to be actively, willfully struggled against in service of love; Tolstoy's teaching, by contrast, begins with human sympathy for suffering and moves toward the world, accepting only what is pleasant and rejecting the rest, producing not a connection between spirit and God but a "loose" connection of "soul with soul in the face of earthly torment."

Historical Context: This is Ilyin's final theological verdict, distinguishing what he calls "true religion" from Tolstoy's sentimental substitute by the direction of their movement — from God outward, versus from man's feelings outward.

Biblical Foundation: The pattern of divine initiative running throughout Scripture — God calling Abraham, God sending the Son into the world He loves despite its fallenness — models exactly the "starts from God, goes to events on earth" movement Ilyin describes as true religion, in contrast to a religion built from human sentiment reaching toward God.

Patristic Witness: The Fathers' consistent insistence that theology (knowledge of God) must precede and ground ethics (right action toward the world), rather than deriving God from human moral sentiment, reflects Ilyin's structural distinction between religion that begins in God and a merely humanistic sympathy dressed in religious language.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin's summary contrast: "true religion accepts the world through will, but does not wholeheartedly accept the evil which arises within it, and therefore conducts a strong-willed, heroic struggle with it," while Tolstoy's teaching "does not even recognize the world because of evil nesting in it" and simply turns away.
  • B. He identifies the object of Tolstoy's "religion" precisely: it "connects not man with god but man with man," and this connection "is composed in mutual sympathy for the burdens of personal life but falls apart in a weak-willed rejection of the general burden at the first sign of true evil."
  • C. Ilyin's closing judgment: this teaching's world-rejection "conceals within itself a rejection of god" — since refusing the world God has made and sustains is, ultimately, a refusal of God's own burden and gift.

Practical Application: A useful test for any spirituality is which direction it moves — does it begin with a vision of God that then sends the believer actively into the world's burdens, or does it begin with personal comfort and sentiment, accepting from "God" only what confirms an already-chosen withdrawal?

Catechumenate Note: This distinguishes authentic Orthodox spirituality, which accepts the world as God's beloved and burdened creation to be actively transfigured, from a spiritualized escapism that uses religious language to justify disengagement from the genuine struggles and responsibilities of communal, worldly life.

Bible Verse Deep Dives

John 3:16

  • Context: The heart of the Gospel — God's love for "the world," sending His Son not to condemn but to save it.
  • Theological Significance: Establishes God's stance toward the fallen world as active, redemptive engagement, not idealization or abandonment.
  • Use in Chapter: Provides the coherent theological alternative to Tolstoy's oscillation between an idyllic world requiring no struggle and a fallen world to be fled entirely.
  • Cross-References: John 1:14; Colossians 1:19-20.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Matthew 25:14-30

  • Context: The parable of the talents, in which the servant who buried his talent out of fear is condemned, while those who risked and invested theirs are commended.
  • Theological Significance: Rejects fearful inaction and appeals to uncertainty as an excuse for failing to act when action was expected.
  • Use in Chapter: Illustrates the failure in Tolstoy's brigand illustration — uncertainty about outcomes is treated by Scripture as no excuse for the "safe" choice of inaction.
  • Cross-References: Luke 19:12-27; Proverbs 24:11-12.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Orthodox Lens

Liturgical Connection: The Church's liturgical affirmation of creation as "very good" (Genesis 1:31) alongside its acknowledgment of the world's fallenness in the Great Litany's petitions "for peace in the whole world" reflects the coherent middle Ilyin says Tolstoy's oscillating cosmology lacks — a world both loved and in need of active, prayerful, and sometimes forceful defense.

Ascetic Formation: Orthodox asceticism, unlike the world-rejection Ilyin critiques, is never a flight from the world's burdens but a disciplined strengthening for bearing them — monastics pray for the whole world precisely because they have not rejected it but taken up its burden in a concentrated form.

Sacramental Theology: The sacraments' affirmation of marriage, childbearing, and material creation (bread, wine, water, oil) as vehicles of grace directly refutes Tolstoy's ascetic rejection of carnality and procreation as inherently corrupting — matter and embodied life are not obstacles to holiness but its ordinary means.

Patristic Harmony: St. Basil the Great's own combination of rigorous asceticism with active, costly engagement in the world's needs (his hospital complex, his defense of the poor against the powerful) embodies exactly the "strong-willed, heroic struggle" Ilyin attributes to true religion, refusing both idyllic complacency and despairing withdrawal.

Thematic Concept Analysis

1. The Incoherence of Selective World-Rejection.
Definition: holding two contradictory religious pictures of the world (divinely harmonious vs. irredeemably fallen) and invoking whichever supports withdrawal in the moment.
Development through the chapter: the paired analysis of Tolstoy's idyllic and fallen visions of nature.
Implications: exposes how apparently pious world-rejection can mask an underlying, prior commitment to passivity rather than a genuine theological conclusion.

2. Selective Appeals to Uncertainty and Providence.
Definition: invoking unknowable consequences or divine will only to justify inaction, never applying the same principle to question whether inaction itself carries risk.
Development: the analysis of Tolstoy's brigand illustration and the reductio of villains' "sacred" ferocity.
Implications: gives a portable test for detecting when appeals to humility or providence are doing selective, rather than genuinely neutral, argumentative work.

3. The Direction of Genuine Religion: From God to the World, Not From Sentiment to God.
Definition: true religion begins in a vision of God that sends the believer actively into the world's burdens; false religion begins in human sentiment and accepts from God only what confirms a prior preference.
Development: Ilyin's closing structural contrast between "true religion" and Tolstoy's "morality of sympathy" dressed as religion.
Implications: offers a lasting diagnostic for distinguishing authentic spirituality from religiously-coded escapism in any era.
