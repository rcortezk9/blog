The Whole Counsel of God: An Integrated Lesson

Introduction: What Is This Book You Are Holding?

Ask an Orthodox Christian "what is the Bible?" and you will not get the answer you'd get from most other Christians. Not "the inerrant Word of God, verbally dictated" and not "an ancient religious text, humanly assembled." Fr. Stephen De Young's The Whole Counsel of God spends five chapters refusing both of those familiar answers, and refusing them in a specific order: first he tells you what kind of thing Scripture is, then what belongs inside it, then how it physically reached you across two or three thousand years, then which English words you should actually be reading, and only at the very end — once all of that is settled — does he tell you how to read it. Each chapter answers the question the last one raises. That structure is the argument. By the time you reach the final page, you are not holding a different opinion about the Bible; you are holding a different Bible.

The whole book turns on one move, made in the first chapter and never abandoned: Scripture and the Holy Spirit's ongoing life in the Church — what Orthodoxy calls Holy Tradition — are not two competing authorities you have to rank. They are two witnesses to the same Person, and per the Law's own rule that a matter is established by two or three witnesses, their testimony has to agree because it is testimony to the same Christ. Once you see that move, everything else in the book — canon, textual criticism, translation, interpretation — is just that same move applied to a new question.

Part I: What Is the Bible? — Two Witnesses, Not One

De Young starts somewhere unexpected: not with what the Bible says, but with how it came to exist at all. The word "inspiration" conjures, for most people raised in a Western Christian framework, a picture of dictation — God speaking, a human hand writing down exactly what it heard, once. The actual compositional history of Scripture doesn't support that picture. The Pentateuch carries Moses's authorship together with later editorial updating (the place-name "Dan" in Genesis 14:14 didn't exist until the book of Judges). Isaiah was collected by disciples after the prophet's own instruction: "bind up the testimony; seal the teaching among my disciples" (Isaiah 8:16). Paul dictated to secretaries, and at least one of his New Testament letters looks like a splice of two. Second Peter 1:21 says the prophets "spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" — carried, not dictated to. The Spirit inspired a centuries-long process of writing, editing, and compiling, the same way grace elsewhere in Orthodox theology works through human nature rather than overriding it.

That reframing does real work almost immediately, because it defuses two arguments that otherwise consume enormous energy. The first is inerrancy. "Inerrancy" as a technical term is not ancient; it was forged in a specifically American, nineteenth- and twentieth-century fight between Protestant fundamentalists and liberals, a fight that grew out of Enlightenment rationalism applied to the biblical text — explaining away Christ's miracles, atomizing the Old Testament into hypothetical sources, hunting for a "historical Jesus" behind "the Christ of faith." The Orthodox Church watched that whole fight from outside it. She has always taught that Scripture doesn't err, but she has never made winning a historical-critical argument against secular scholarship a precondition of faith. You are not required, as an Orthodox Christian, to defend six-day creation or Exodus troop numbers to remain faithful. Scripture's trustworthiness rests on its place inside the Church's worshiping life, not on a debate with archaeology.

The second argument is sola scriptura — "Scripture alone." De Young's point here is sharper than it first sounds: Orthodoxy does not reject sola scriptura the way you'd reject a losing position in a debate you're still having. She rejects the framework that makes the question meaningful in the first place. Both classical Protestantism and Roman Catholicism agree that there are three potentially competing sources of authority — Scripture, tradition, and a teaching magisterium — and disagree only about which one outranks the others. Orthodoxy doesn't play that game, because she doesn't experience Scripture and Tradition as separate things that could, in principle, disagree. Christ names two witnesses who will testify to Him after His ascension — the Spirit of Truth, and the apostles themselves, "because you have been with me from the beginning" (John 15:26–27) — and under the Law's own two-witness rule (Deuteronomy 19:15), for that testimony to count, it has to be identical. It has to be, because both witnesses are pointing at the same living Christ. Scripture functions organically within Holy Tradition, not above it and not beneath it.

That is the chapter's real destination, and it's worth sitting with the shift it represents: the West largely frames knowing God as an epistemological project — assembling correct propositions from natural revelation, Scripture, and tradition. The East frames it as participation — knowing Christ the way you know a person, through union with Him, in the Spirit, inside His Church. Scripture serves that personal knowing. It doesn't compete with it. Which is why, if you're a catechumen preparing for Chrismation, this chapter is quietly naming what your whole catechesis is training you toward: not mastery of correct propositions about God, but Spirit-enabled knowledge of the God those propositions are about.

Part II: What Is in the Bible? — A Canon That Was Received, Not Decided

If Scripture and Tradition are two witnesses to one Christ, the next question is unavoidable: which books actually count as that testimony? Here De Young spends a chapter clearing away two myths, because both of them assume the same wrong picture — that "the Bible" was a finished, agreed-upon object waiting to be either adopted or voted into being, rather than something the Church gradually, organically received.

The first myth is that there was a single, settled "Hebrew Bible" already in existence before Christianity, which Christians simply inherited and then supplemented. There wasn't. Before the codex, Old Testament books circulated as individual scrolls; a small synagogue might own only the Torah, a wealthy one might add Psalms and Isaiah, and only major centers like Jerusalem or Alexandria held anything close to a full range of texts. First-century Judaism had no master list — the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran preserve an entire alternative community's canon, containing far more copies of 1 Enoch than of Genesis or Exodus, and missing Esther entirely. The historian Josephus, a Pharisee, presents his own party's twenty-two-book list in Against Apion as though every Jew agreed with it. He was speaking for a faction, not a consensus. The Old Testament the Church received was not a borrowed, finished artifact; it was the Church's own reception, through the apostles, of a genuinely varied, living Second Temple inheritance.

That reception did settle into a real shape — a shared core of the familiar books across all Orthodox local churches, plus seven further texts (Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Ben Sirach, Baruch, the Epistle of Jeremiah) — but even beyond that core, minor variation between local churches was never something the East felt compelled to forcibly resolve. St. Athanasius, in his Paschal Letter 39, placed several additional texts into a "second rank" — not banned, not read publicly in church, but not discarded either. That three-tier pastoral logic — read in church, read at home, not read — let real variation exist without anyone needing to win a fight about it. The functional gap between "canonical" and "deuterocanonical," De Young says plainly, is negligible in actual Orthodox practice. You are not being handed a checklist to memorize. You are being catechized into a logic of reception.

The second myth is more familiar and more wrong: that the twenty-seven-book New Testament was voted into existence at the Council of Nicea in 325, with a Constantinian book-burning to enforce the result. Nicea never discussed the canon. The real story is almost the opposite of centralized control — by roughly AD 150, the four Gospels were already being bound together and used exclusively in major churches like Rome and Antioch, not because those cities held special authority but because their communities held living memory of which texts the apostles had actually delivered to them. Paul's epistles were circulating as a collected unit by AD 100. St. Irenaeus, writing in the second century, could already date Mark's Gospel with precision and recognized Gnostic communities as non-Christian partly because they used a different set of texts — canon functioning as a marker of identity, not a scholar's literary judgment. Nineteen of the twenty-seven books were never seriously disputed at all. The remaining eight — the General Epistles and Revelation — weren't rejected so much as unevenly known: a community that had only ever received one letter of John had simply never encountered the other two. The very term "disputed books" is, De Young argues, a modern mistranslation of Eusebius, who was only distinguishing books everyone said the same thing about from books people described differently — not books formally voted down and later reinstated.

The chapter's closing line is its real thesis, and it's the hinge the whole book turns on: "the only entity that chose the books that would comprise our New Testament is the Holy Spirit... developed in the shared life of the Christian people, which the Orthodox Church calls Holy Tradition." Canon formation isn't a closed, finished event in the past. It's the paradigm case of the same Spirit who continues to guide the Church into all truth.

Part III: How Did the Bible Get to Me? — Losing the Mirage of an "Original"

Once you know the canon was received rather than decided, a harder question follows: even granting that these are the right books, what are the actual words? Here De Young takes aim at an assumption so common most readers never notice they're making it — that somewhere out there is a perfect "original text," lost but in principle recoverable, and that textual scholarship's job is to dig it up. He calls it a mirage, and spends the chapter's most technical material showing why.

The 1978 Chicago Statement on Inerrancy claimed an inspired, inerrant original text exists and "can now be established with a high degree of confidence using modern tools." Follow that claim to its conclusion and it collapses: it would mean the Church possessed no fully trustworthy Bible for most of her two-thousand-year history, since that kind of critical reconstruction is a distinctly modern academic achievement. Neither the first-century form of a text nor a present-day scholarly reconstruction of it can be privileged over the actual copies the Church has read and prayed with for two millennia. Every generation, in every tribe and tongue, received the Scriptures the Spirit intended them to have.

The evidence bears this out in two very different ways for the two Testaments. The Hebrew Masoretic tradition was preserved with extraordinary, almost obsessive precision — vowel markings, word counts for copy verification — and when the Dead Sea Scrolls surfaced, they matched texts written over a millennium earlier with nearly 100 percent agreement. But the Masoretic Text isn't the only ancient witness, and where it diverges from the Greek Old Testament — properly called the Old Greek, since "Septuagint" strictly names only the Torah's translation — the Greek frequently preserves an older Hebrew reading, not a mistranslation. Roughly 70 percent of the New Testament's Old Testament quotations come from the Greek, not the Hebrew directly, which means the New Testament's own theological vocabulary was shaped by this specific tradition. The starkest case is Daniel: the Church's liturgically canonical Greek text of Daniel comes not from the ancient Old Greek translation (nearly lost — only two manuscripts survive) but from a non-Christian second-century Jewish scholar named Theodotion, whose version simply displaced it in Christian use. What the Church actually received and continued to pray with became the canonical form, chronological priority notwithstanding. The Song of the Three Youths, chanted every Holy Saturday, comes from exactly this Theodotion tradition.

For the New Testament, the argument runs the opposite direction — not one disciplined tradition but overwhelming abundance, nearly 5,800 Greek manuscripts, the earliest fragment dating within decades of composition. Set beside the rest of the ancient world, this is almost absurdly well-attested: Aristotle's Constitution of Athens survives in four manuscripts, Tacitus's Annals in two. The oft-cited alarming statistic — more variants than words in the New Testament — is true and almost entirely trivial: the overwhelming majority are spelling errors, and of what remains, none touches a single point of Christian doctrine. The reason no one could ever centrally corrupt the New Testament text is precisely that no one ever held that kind of centralized control over it — the abundance itself, spread across the whole ancient world in Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Latin, and Church Fathers' own quotations, is the safeguard.

Textual criticism, done rightly, isn't a corrosive, suspicious discipline. It's an act of gratitude for a genuinely rich inheritance — a discipline that becomes possible, and possible to enjoy, only once you've released the mirage of the single perfect original and start appreciating what you actually have.

Part IV: Which Bible Should I Read? — Translation as an Interpretive Act

Having established what the Bible is, what belongs in it, and how its text survived transmission, De Young turns to the question every English-speaking Orthodox Christian eventually has to answer for themselves: which translation do I actually pick up? His opening premise sets the terms for everything that follows — every translation is an interpretation, whether the translator intends that or not.

Two independent choices sit behind any English Bible. The first is base text — which underlying Hebrew or Greek document a translation actually renders. For the Old Testament, most English Bibles use the Masoretic Text; anything beyond the Rabbinic canon has to come from the Greek tradition instead. For the New Testament, "eclectic texts" compiled from multiple manuscripts have been standard since Erasmus first published a printed Greek New Testament from just eight incomplete manuscripts in the sixteenth century — a text later refined into the Textus Receptus behind the King James Version, and later still displaced by Westcott and Hort's 1881 shift toward the Alexandrian text type. No edition since has ever been declared finished or inspired; each is a stage in an ongoing process, exactly like the transmission history Chapter 3 described.

The second choice is translation philosophy — where a given Bible sits on the spectrum from formal equivalence (word-for-word fidelity, even at the cost of natural English) to dynamic equivalence (sense-for-sense readability, at the cost of one more layer of built-in interpretation), with paraphrases forming an entirely separate and, for Orthodox purposes, unsuitable category. A paraphrase isn't a fresh translation from the original languages at all — it's a rewording of an existing English translation — and in simplifying, paraphrases like The Message routinely blur exactly the Trinitarian and Christological precision that matters most. To read a paraphrase, De Young insists, "is not to read the Scriptures, but to read someone's interpretation of the Scriptures."

For Orthodox readers specifically wanting the Greek Old Testament in English, there are really only four real options, each imperfect in a different way: Brenton's, historically important but written in a deliberately archaic style never meant to stand alone; the Orthodox Study Bible, the most readable but translated inconsistently book-to-book by different hands; the NETS, precise but built for scholarship rather than devotion; and the Lexham English Septuagint, which — in a small but telling irony — faithfully translates liturgical material bound into its source manuscript (the biblical odes) without recognizing that material as liturgical at all, a reminder of what happens when Scripture gets approached as a purely literary artifact apart from the Church's worshiping life. Among full English Bibles, De Young's own recommendation lands on the ESV as the most accurate and readable option for daily reading, descended from the RSV — the first English translation to receive genuine Orthodox input — while treating even flawed, doctrinally slanted options like the NIV with real charity: even an imperfect translation still lets "an Orthodox Christian hear their voice." You are never asked to feel spiritually endangered by whatever Bible first formed your faith. You are only invited, gently, toward something better.

Part V: How Should I Read My Bible? — Guided Reading, Held Together

Everything in the first four chapters — what Scripture is, what belongs in it, how it survived, which words to actually read — exists to answer the one question an ordinary believer actually lives with: given all that, how do I read this well, today? De Young diagnoses the common failure first. Most "read the Bible in a year" plans don't fail from laziness; they fail because Genesis holds a reader's interest until the tabernacle measurements in the back half of Exodus, and then Leviticus's ritual law finishes the job — a single undifferentiated reading strategy applied to a library that was never meant to be read that way.

His alternative is a three-stage discipline: first establish what the text says — a step complicated, but not defeated, by the translation and manuscript questions of the two previous chapters; then determine what it means, by reading outward through three widening layers of context — the immediate surrounding passage, the historical moment (sometimes two moments at once, since Abraham's era and Moses's later editorial hand are different centuries entirely), and the whole canon read as one mutually illuminating library; and only then ask how it applies to an actual life. That third step is where the chapter does its most important work. Application, De Young insists, is not something an individual reader improvises from scratch. "If my understanding of how a portion of Scripture should be applied is found nowhere among the saints and holy Fathers who came before us, then I am wrong" — a genuinely strong claim, immediately balanced by an equally sharp caution: finding one Father, or several, who happen to agree with your reading is no guarantee you're right either. The real posture he's describing is neither parroting the Fathers nor discarding them for private insight, but what he calls the realm of continuity — reading and living Scripture the way the Fathers did, adapted, necessarily, to a different time and place, without becoming a genuinely different faith.

The governing image for the whole chapter is the Ethiopian eunuch of Acts 8 — sincere, literate, reading Isaiah 53 in his chariot, and honest enough to answer Philip's question with "How can I understand, unless someone guides me?" Needing a guide isn't a failure of independent faith. It's the biblical pattern itself. And the chapter's final, most significant move corrects a genuinely widespread myth: that the Fathers split neatly into a "literal" Antiochene school and an "allegorical" Alexandrian one. De Young shows this to be a modern anachronism, a retrojection of Chapter 1's fundamentalist-liberal fight backward onto history where it doesn't belong. Every Father, without exception, held two things together rather than choosing between them — the real, literal, historical sense of a text (Abraham genuinely lived; the Exodus genuinely happened) and its Christological sense, the way that same text points toward and is fulfilled in Christ (the Exodus as a type of Baptism, per 1 Corinthians 10:1–4). Deny the literal sense, and nothing built on top of it is trustworthy. Stop at the literal sense alone, and Scripture becomes a historical curiosity with nothing to say to anyone born after the first century. Both readings are required, simultaneously, by the same text — and that refusal to choose a false binary is, De Young shows, the same move this entire book has made from its opening page: Scripture and Spirit, canon received and not decided, transmission through abundance and not a single guarded copy, translation held with more than one voice and not anxious certainty in exactly one. The book closes where it began — Scripture is not information to master, but, in De Young's own words, "what we inhale and exhale to breathe and spiritually live."

Conclusion: One Whole Counsel

Read straight through, the five chapters aren't five separate essays loosely bound by a shared author. They're one sustained argument, each stage necessary for the next to make sense. You cannot ask what belongs in the Bible until you know what kind of thing the Bible is — a Spirit-carried process, not a dictation. You cannot ask how the text reached you until you know the canon itself was received rather than decided, because the same organic, Spirit-guided reception that gathered the books also governed which words within them survived. You cannot choose a translation intelligently until you understand that the text you're translating was never a single, final, recoverable original to begin with. And none of the historical and text-critical groundwork in the first four chapters matters at all if the final chapter's discipline — reading, interpreting, and applying in continuity with the Fathers rather than alone — never gets practiced.

The title names the whole argument, if you let it. This is not a book about defending "the whole Bible" against skeptics, verse by verse. It's a book about receiving "the whole counsel of God" (Acts 20:27) — the entire, layered, historically real, communally received, Spirit-carried testimony to Jesus Christ, in the only place that testimony has ever actually lived: the worshiping, praying, Spirit-indwelt Church that produced it, preserved it, translated it, and is still, today, teaching you how to read it.

Reflection Questions

  1. De Young's opening move — Scripture and the Spirit's life in the Church as "two witnesses" to one Christ rather than two competing authorities — is meant to dissolve the sola scriptura debate rather than win it. Having read all five chapters, do you find that the same move actually resolves the canon question (Ch. 2), the "original text" question (Ch. 3), and the translation question (Ch. 4) as well? Or does it work better in some chapters than others?

  2. Chapter 2 argues that canon is "received, not decided" — an organic, communal, centuries-long process rather than a single authoritative act. What would change about how you talk to a skeptical friend about "who chose the Bible" if you led with this framing instead of defending a fixed list?

  3. The Theodotion Daniel case (Ch. 3) shows the Church's canonical text coming from a non-Christian scholar's translation, chronologically later than — and eventually replacing — an earlier Christian one. What does this suggest about where scriptural authority actually resides: in a text's origin, or in the Church's reception and continued use of it?

  4. Chapter 4 insists "every translation is an interpretation" and recommends reading more than one. Which English translation(s) have actually shaped your own reading of Scripture so far, and has anything in this synthesis changed which one(s) you'd reach for next?

  5. Chapter 5's strongest claim — "if my understanding of how a portion of Scripture should be applied is found nowhere among the saints and Fathers, then I am wrong" — is immediately qualified by the warning that a single supporting Father is no guarantee of being right either. How do you tell the difference, in your own reading, between genuine continuity with the Fathers and citation-hunting to confirm what you already believed?

  6. The book corrects a "literal vs. allegorical Fathers" myth by showing both senses were always held together. Try it yourself: take one Old Testament narrative you know well and articulate both its literal-historical sense and a legitimate Christological sense, and notice whether one genuinely strengthens the other or whether you're forcing a connection.
