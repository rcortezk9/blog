#asceticism#book/on_resistance_to_evil_by_force#book_study#moral_theology#orthodox#theology

"Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy. But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you..."
— Matthew 5:43-44

Before you read: This chapter takes on what is probably the single strongest scriptural objection to the whole book: doesn't Christ command us to love our enemies and not resist evil? Ilyin's answer turns on two careful distinctions he asks you to hold simultaneously — between personal enemies and the enemies of God, and between forgiving a personal grievance and resolving the separate, larger problem of a villain's ongoing crime against others. Read the hermit-and-raven legend slowly; it is the chapter's emotional center and its clearest warning against mistaking sentimental pity for genuine love.

Chapter Overview

Ilyin argues that spiritual love, precisely because it is a "clear-sighted living substantive choice" and not a formless overflow of feeling, has real limits — it is psychologically and spiritually impossible to love everything or everyone equally, and no one is called to love evil as such, or the devil, with "creative acceptance." One may desire evil's transfiguration and even pray for it, but sympathizing with evil's goals, uniting one's work with it, or extending it loving identification would itself be a fall. He illustrates the danger with a stark legend: a hermit who resisted the devil in every disguise finally succumbs when the devil appears as a wounded, suffering raven, and "blind sentimental compassion" — love mistaking evil's disguise for a fellow-sufferer — undoes years of resistance in a moment. Ilyin then addresses the chapter's central scriptural challenge directly: Christ's command to "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:43-44) concerns personal enemies — those who have wronged me — not the enemies of God, whom Christ Himself confronted with "fiery words of reproof," threats of judgment, and the driving-out scourge in the Temple. A Christian is never called to manufacture tenderness toward an unrepentant villain as such, only to forgive personal injury and to wage necessary resistance "not as with personal enemies but as with enemies of the cause of God" — and paradoxically, the less personal hatred remains in the resister's heart, the more faithful and effective this resistance can be. Ilyin's sharpest and most practically important move follows: personal forgiveness (which every Christian is called to extend, without limit, for injuries done to himself) extinguishes the offense's power in the forgiver's own heart, but it does not and cannot resolve the separate, larger problem of the offender's continuing evil against others, since "who gave me the right to forgive, by myself, the villains who... bring about the death of the homeland?" That larger problem belongs to society, law, and God — not to any private individual's unilateral pardon. Finally, Ilyin reads "resist not evil" (Matthew 5:39) narrowly and correctly, as applying to personal grievances and property, never as license for the "inactive contemplation of violations and injustices" against others — since Christ Himself, per Romans 13:4 and the Church's own tradition (Ilyin cites St. Theodosius of the Kiev Caves), affirms that "God's servants... [bear] the sword... not in vain."

Main Points

1. Love Cannot Extend to Evil as Such, or to the Devil, With Creative Acceptance

Core Argument: Spiritual love, as a substantive and discriminating choice rather than an indiscriminate feeling, is incapable of embracing everything equally — no one is called to love evil as such, and love of the devil, understood as loving identification and acceptance of his goals, "must be recognized as completely unnatural"; one may desire evil's transfiguration and pray even for the devil, but this differs entirely from sympathetic, creative union with evil's purposes.

Historical Context: Ilyin is directly countering a possible misreading of Chapter 14's account of love as universal benevolence — clarifying that willing everyone's good (in the sense of desiring the defeat of evil in them) is not the same as loving evil itself or extending creative acceptance to it.

Biblical Foundation: James 4:7's command to "resist the devil" presupposes exactly the limit Ilyin describes — Scripture never commands loving submission or creative acceptance toward the source of evil, only resistance to it.

Patristic Witness: The desert Fathers' teaching on discerning and firmly rejecting demonic suggestion, rather than entertaining it with sympathetic curiosity, embodies precisely Ilyin's distinction between benevolent hope for transfiguration and the "loving acceptance" that would constitute spiritual capitulation.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin allows that a spiritually strong person might deliberately confront evil "for purposes of trial, knowledge, and wisdom," but insists this "will never turn into a loving acceptance" if undertaken rightly — it remains painful, "repulsive," and purposed solely toward resistance.
  • B. He warns this kind of trial "can become an excessive temptation" for a weak person, risking a fall "not only due to... blind or naive infection by evil but also because of an incorrect comprehension of the limits of love."
  • C. The legend of the hermit and the wounded raven crystallizes the danger: sentimental compassion, untested by spiritual discernment, opened the door to the very evil the hermit had long resisted.

Practical Application: Compassion toward someone or something presenting as wounded, suffering, or sympathetic should still be tested by spiritual discernment before being extended fully — genuine love does not require suspending judgment about what is actually being invited in.

Catechumenate Note: This is directly relevant to discerning temptation in one's own spiritual life — the raven legend is a vivid warning against mistaking a plausible, sympathetic disguise for something deserving full trust and acceptance, a discernment the Church's ascetic tradition trains through vigilance (nepsis) and obedience to spiritual guidance.

2. Christ's Command to Love Enemies Concerns Personal Enemies, Not the Enemies of God

Core Argument: Christ's command to "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:43-47) addresses a person's own personal haters and persecutors, whom the offended party may choose to forgive — it does not command love, blessing, or sympathetic assistance toward the enemies of God, blasphemers, or those who prey on the vulnerable, since Christ Himself confronted such figures with fierce reproof, warnings of judgment, and even the driving-out scourge in the Temple.

Historical Context: This is Ilyin's central answer to what he treats as the book's most serious scriptural objection — if Christ commands universal love of enemies, how can force against the wicked ever be justified? Ilyin resolves this by distinguishing the category of "enemy" at stake in each case.

Biblical Foundation: Matthew 5:43-47's command to love personal enemies is set directly against Christ's own extensive record of confrontation — "fiery words of reproof" (Matthew 11:21-24; 23; Mark 12:38-40; Luke 11:39-52), threats of judgment (Matthew 10:15; 12:9; 21:41; 22:7; 24:51; 25:12, 30), the cleansing of the Temple (Matthew 21:12; Mark 11:15; Luke 19:45; John 2:13-16), and warnings of eternal torment (Matthew 25:41, 46; John 5:29) — all directed at those Ilyin calls enemies of God rather than merely His own personal offenders.

Patristic Witness: St. Theodosius of the Kiev Caves' teaching (quoted directly by Ilyin) — "live in peace not only with your friends but also with your enemies, but only your personal enemies, and not the enemies of God" — supplies the exact distinction Ilyin builds his argument upon, drawn from within the Orthodox monastic tradition itself.

Sub-Points:

  • A. A Christian resisting evildoers should wage this struggle "not as with personal enemies but as with enemies of the cause of God on earth," and paradoxically, the less personal hatred remains, the more faithful and effective the resistance.
  • B. Ilyin insists this in no way weakens Christ's call to forgive personal grievances — "he who resists evil must forgive personal grievances, and the more sincere and complete this forgiveness, the more such a person is capable of leading an impersonal, objective struggle."
  • C. He explicitly denies that this distinction is any kind of "objection to the commandments of Christ" — rather, failing to make it "would only testify to the inadequate thoughtfulness of the person making it."

Practical Application: When facing genuine wrongdoing directed at others (not merely oneself), the call to "love your enemy" does not require manufacturing personal affection for the wrongdoer — it requires forgiving whatever personal offense one may separately hold, while still resisting the wrong itself without personal hatred clouding the resistance.

Catechumenate Note: This distinction is pastorally essential for anyone struggling with guilt over feeling that resistance to evil (defending the vulnerable, opposing injustice) seems to contradict Christian love — the Church's own tradition, exemplified by St. Theodosius, has always drawn exactly this line between personal and impersonal enmity.

3. Personal Forgiveness Extinguishes Malice in the Forgiver's Heart — It Does Not Resolve the Offender's Continuing Evil

Core Argument: The command to forgive personal grievances "without limit" (Matthew 18:21-22) genuinely extinguishes the offense's power over the one who forgives, but it does not and cannot resolve the separate, larger problem of the offender's ongoing villainy against others, society, and God — since "who gave me the right to forgive, by myself, the villains who actively profane holy things or practice the vicious seduction of minors" — that jurisdiction belongs to the Church, the state, and God, not to any private person's unilateral pardon.

Historical Context: This is arguably the chapter's most practically important distinction, since it directly resolves the common conflation of "I forgive you" (a personal, spiritual act available to any victim) with "your crime is resolved" (a social, legal, and religious matter far beyond any individual's authority).

Biblical Foundation: Matthew 18:15-17's procedure for addressing sin against the community — private correction escalating to "the church" as final earthly authority — presupposes exactly the distinction Ilyin draws: personal forgiveness (available immediately, per Matthew 18:21-22) coexists with, and does not replace, the community's separate responsibility to address ongoing wrongdoing.

Patristic Witness: The Church's sacramental distinction between personal reconciliation (forgiving one's offender in one's heart) and canonical discipline (which only a priest with the authority to absolve, or the Church's governing structures, can properly administer) mirrors precisely Ilyin's claim that "he is not a priest with the authority to shrive the penitent, nor the supreme state body with the authority to grant amnesty" for crimes against others.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin's key formula: "forgiveness is the first condition of the fight against evil... but not the end and not a victory... it is truly necessary to have no less than 12 legions of angels" (Matthew 26:53) before a real villain would ever mistake mere forgiveness for anything but "encouragement."
  • B. He insists the existence of a villain "is a problem not in the least for one victim" alone, but "for all," and therefore the victim too — "not considered as a victim... but as a member of that social union" called to the "organized struggle against evil."
  • C. The false conflation Ilyin rejects directly: treating personal forgiveness as equivalent to "I do not condemn or accuse," or "I reconcile myself with their crimes and undertake not to interfere with them" — this, he says, "has nothing to do with Christian forgiveness and cannot be justified by any references to the Gospel."

Practical Application: A victim of wrongdoing can and should forgive the personal injury done to them — releasing personal bitterness — while still supporting, or even actively pursuing, the wrongdoer's accountability through proper social, legal, or ecclesiastical channels; these are not in tension but operate on entirely different planes.

Catechumenate Note: This is an important pastoral clarification for anyone who has been taught (or has assumed) that "forgiving" someone who has done serious wrong means one must also refrain from reporting the wrong, seeking justice, or supporting appropriate consequences — the Church's own canonical and confessional structure has always maintained this distinction between personal reconciliation and the community's separate responsibility.

4. "Resist Not Evil" Concerns Personal Property and Grievance, Not the Defense of Others

Core Argument: Christ's teaching "resist not evil" (Matthew 5:39) — read in context alongside its call to generosity with one's own goods and gentleness in personal matters — governs a person's response to injury against himself and his own property, not a general prohibition on defending others or resisting injustice; reading it as license for "inactive contemplation of violations and injustices" toward others would make Christ's own teaching "tantamount to hypocritical righteousness and complicity with the evildoers."

Historical Context: Ilyin closes the chapter by directly confronting the single verse most often cited against the entire book's project, insisting a careful reading actually supports rather than undermines his argument.

Biblical Foundation: Romans 13:4's declaration that the governing authority "beareth not the sword in vain" is cited by Ilyin as the apostolic and patristic counter-witness confirming that "resist not evil" was never understood by the Church to abolish the sword's legitimate function against wrongdoing.

Patristic Witness: St. Theodosius of the Kiev Caves' distinction (already invoked above) between personal peace with one's own enemies and the impossibility of peace with "the enemies of God" gives an authoritative Orthodox monastic voice to precisely this reading of "resist not evil" as bounded by context, not universal in scope.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin poses the reductio directly: "is it fair to betray the weak to the villain in order to show gentleness? ...should the villains be given the freedom to burn temples, plant atheism, and destroy their homeland? Is this what it means to be gentle and generous?"
  • B. He insists Christ's actual teaching and example — combined with "the teaching of the apostles and fathers of the Church" — "advanced a completely different understanding" than blanket non-resistance.
  • C. The chapter's closing thesis, restated: "the beginning of the spirit limits the scope of love in its immediate, naive overflow" — love is not diminished but rightly bounded by spiritual discernment of what love actually requires in each case.

Practical Application: When Christ's teaching on non-retaliation is invoked to justify standing by while a third party is harmed, it is worth asking whether the passage is being read in its actual context (personal injury, personal property) or being stretched into a universal principle it was never meant to establish.

Catechumenate Note: This equips a catechumen to respond thoughtfully when "turn the other cheek" is invoked (by others, or in one's own conscience) as a reason not to defend someone vulnerable — the Church's own tradition has consistently read this teaching as governing personal injury, not as forbidding the defense of the innocent.

Bible Verse Deep Dives

Matthew 5:43-47

  • Context: Part of the Sermon on the Mount's teaching on love of enemies, following the "eye for an eye" teaching on personal retaliation.
  • Theological Significance: Commands transformative love toward one's own personal persecutors, modeling divine impartiality (God sends rain "on the just and on the unjust").
  • Use in Chapter: Anchors Ilyin's central distinction — this command concerns personal enemies, not the enemies of God, whom Christ Himself confronted very differently.
  • Cross-References: Luke 6:27-28; Romans 12:20; Luke 23:34.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Matthew 18:15-22

  • Context: Christ's teaching on addressing sin within the community, escalating from private correction to the whole church, immediately followed by the teaching on limitless forgiveness.
  • Theological Significance: Holds together, in a single teaching unit, both the call to personal forgiveness and the community's structured responsibility to address ongoing wrongdoing.
  • Use in Chapter: Provides the direct scriptural basis for Ilyin's distinction between the forgiveness available to any victim and the separate, communal responsibility to address a wrongdoer's continuing sin.
  • Cross-References: Luke 17:3-4; Matthew 6:14-15.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Orthodox Lens

Liturgical Connection: The Church's practice of the "Kiss of Peace" and mutual forgiveness (especially the Forgiveness Vespers opening Great Lent) exemplifies the personal forgiveness Ilyin describes as unconditionally required, existing alongside, and never abolishing, the Church's separate canonical structures for addressing serious wrongdoing.

Ascetic Formation: The desert tradition's teaching on discerning demonic suggestion — testing every thought and appearance rather than extending automatic sympathy — embodies exactly the vigilance the hermit-and-raven legend warns is necessary; sentimental compassion untested by discernment is a recognized ascetic hazard, not a hypothetical concern.

Sacramental Theology: The distinction between the priest's sacramental authority to absolve sins confessed against God and the state's or Church's separate authority to address crimes against others reflects Ilyin's insistence that personal forgiveness and the resolution of a wrongdoer's guilt before the community operate on entirely different planes.

Patristic Harmony: St. Theodosius of the Kiev Caves' explicit distinction between personal enemies and enemies of God gives this chapter's central argument direct patristic grounding within the Orthodox tradition itself, not merely as Ilyin's own philosophical construction.

Thematic Concept Analysis

1. The Impossibility of Loving Evil as Such.
Definition: spiritual love, being discriminating rather than indiscriminate, cannot extend creative acceptance to evil or the devil.
Development through the chapter: the legend of the hermit and the wounded raven as a warning against mistaking sentimental pity for genuine spiritual discernment.
Implications: distinguishes healthy compassion (tested by discernment) from a naive openness that can itself become the avenue of spiritual defeat.

2. Personal Enemies Versus Enemies of God.
Definition: Christ's command to love enemies addresses personal persecutors, not the enemies of God's cause on earth, whom Christ Himself confronted with reproof and judgment.
Development: the extended catalogue of Christ's own confrontational words and actions set against His command to love personal enemies.
Implications: resolves the apparent tension between "love your enemies" and the book's defense of resistance to evildoers.

3. Personal Forgiveness Versus Communal Resolution of Wrongdoing.
Definition: forgiving a personal grievance extinguishes its power in the forgiver's own heart but does not resolve the wrongdoer's ongoing guilt or danger to others.
Development: Ilyin's direct refutation of conflating "I forgive you" with "your crime is resolved" or "I will not interfere."
Implications: liberates victims to forgive personally while still supporting justice, protection, and accountability for others.
