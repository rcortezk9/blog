Text Analysis: Thinking Orthodox, Ch. 3 — Phronema in the Western Christian Tradition

Scan note: Rebuilt 2026-08-26 in the two-tier format. Pages 36–43 and 45–56 are clean and quoted verbatim; p. 46 (the Aquinas/Palamas contrast) was re-shot with vFlat on 2026-08-26 and is now quoted directly. Page 44 is still outstanding — it was cropped in the earlier photo scans and was not included in the vFlat batch, so the Augustine-to-Anselm trajectory remains paraphrase-only and is not quoted anywhere in this note. One page to re-shoot.

Curated Takeaways

Core Claims

  • "This is because that which shapes one's theological perspective is not the Scriptures themselves but the mindset through which one reads and applies those Scriptures." — The chapter's governing thesis, and the answer to why shared Scriptures have never produced shared belief.
  • "Sola scriptura is the most fundamental and foundational principle of the Protestant mindset: one can determine for oneself what the Scriptures mean. The claim, therefore, is in effect not just 'the Scriptures alone' but 'myself alone.'" — Her sharpest reframing of the Protestant principle: the practical outcome of removing every interpretive authority is the elevation of the individual.
  • "If Sola Scriptura is the bedrock of Protestant Christianity, 'faith and reason' is the mantra and driving principle of Catholic theology." — Sets up the chapter's parallel structure and names the single Catholic commitment from which the other three aspects follow.
  • "Augustine initiated the trajectory that ultimately resulted in the schism between East and West. He sowed the seeds of a different phronema, which over ensuing centuries gradually grew in the West due to his immense influence." — The historical hinge of the whole chapter: the divide begins not with 1054 but with a method of doing theology.
  • "The Orthodox think of sin not as an offense against God that demands punishment or restitution, but primarily as an illness that needs healing." — The soteriological payoff — legal versus medical categories — which explains purgatory, merit, indulgences, and penance in one move.

Supporting Evidence

  • Source: Catechism of the Catholic Church I.1.III.36, citing Vatican I, Dei Filius
    • "Our holy mother, the Church, holds and teaches that God, the first principle and last end of all things, can be known with certainty from the created world by the natural light of human reason." — Official text establishing that the Catholic claim goes beyond "creation reveals God exists" to actual knowledge of God attained by reasoning.
  • Source: Catholic Code of Canon Law, Canon 213
    • "The faithful therefore have the right to be instructed in the divine saving precepts that purify judgment and, with grace, heal wounded human reason." — Her key catch: what needs healing in the Catholic frame is the faculty of reason, not the soul — so wrong belief is a reasoning error.
  • Source: Lumen Gentium 25 (Vatican II)
    • "This loyal submission of the will and intellect must be given in a special way to the authentic teaching authority of the Roman Pontiff, even when he does not speak ex cathedra" — Documents that required submission extends past infallible pronouncements to ordinary papal teaching.
  • Source: Pope Paul VI, Indulgentiarum Doctrina 1.2–3
    • "It is a divinely revealed truth that sins bring punishments inflicted by God's sanctity and justice. These must be expiated either on this earth... or else in the life beyond through fire and torments or 'purifying' punishments." — The clearest official statement of the debt model: guilt may be forgiven while the penalty remains outstanding.
  • Source: Joseph C. McLelland, "Sailing to Byzantium"
    • "Both 'Roman and Reformed were content to argue about the same problems—neither side questioned the agenda. Both shared the assumptions of Western theology.'" — Establishes the chapter's structural point: Protestant and Catholic are rival answers inside one shared phronema, not two phronemas.
  • Source: Cardinal John Henry Newman, The Development of Christian Doctrine
    • "Newman describes the work of the Fathers of the Church almost entirely as a process of thought: 'fixing their minds' on a question, 'grasping' the concepts, 'viewing' various sides, 'weighing' possibilities." — Shows development of doctrine framed as intellectual labor, with prayer and relationship to God barely mentioned.

Key Terminology

  • Sola scriptura: "it is not only the Scriptures alone, but the Scriptures apart—apart from the Church, from any church authority or the authority of traditional interpretation."
  • Dikaiosyne: the Greek behind "justification" actually means "righteousness," which "hints at virtue and right conduct, including practices of piety" — translating it as "justification" promotes a legalistic, transactional view.
  • Potuit, decuit, fecit: "God (or Christ) could do something; it was fitting that he should; therefore, he did it."
  • Merit: "Christ and the saints have a reward owed to them by God, and we can tap into their accomplishments to receive a benefit" — a concept "totally absent from the Orthodox."
  • Imprimatur / nihil obstat: the official license to publish Catholic books, and the words "nothing hinders" on the title page verso.
  • Development of doctrine: the Catholic teaching that "as the centuries succeed one another, the Church constantly progresses towards the fullness of divine truth."

Synthesis & Application

  • Conclusion: "Thus, it is evident that the gulf between the Orthodox and Western Christianity in the critical matter of sin and forgiveness is enormous."
  • Application: "Furthermore, sacramental forgiveness in the Orthodox Church is always complete and immediate. The relationship with God is completely restored with no penalty remaining to be paid."

Full Chapter Facts

(Organized by the chapter's own sections, in source order. One compact line per fact. All pages clean except p. 44, which is still awaiting a rescan and remains paraphrase-only.)

Opening — everyone has a phronema (p. 36)

  • "Everyone has a phronema, a particular attitude or approach not only to theology but to life in general."
  • The Greek roots linking thought and conduct show that a true Orthodox phronema leads to correct Orthodox behavior.
  • Parallel drawn to the word orthodox itself, meaning both "correct belief or opinion" and "correct glory or worship."
  • Fasting returns as the test case: rigidity and a pharisaical approach fail to display an Orthodox phronema.
  • But "ignoring, minimizing, or marginalizing fasting equally indicates a lack of Orthodox phronema."
  • Balance — "deviating neither to the right nor to the left" — is seen in the Fathers' teaching and spiritual advice.
  • Especially associated with St. Gregory the Theologian, grounded in Deut. and Josh. 1:7 injunctions.
  • Correct phronema is "particularly essential for the Orthodox theologian," governing what he will say, do, and write.

The Protestant Phronema: Sola Scriptura (p. 37)

  • Restates Paul's call to unity of thought and mind within the Church.
  • Poses the chapter's driving question: all Christians hold the New Testament authoritative, so why no shared phronema?
  • Paul's directives "have not created Christian unity, even for those who attempt to follow his scriptural directives."
  • Luther "was less extreme than his followers and maintained many traditional Catholic views."
  • Sola scriptura nevertheless became "the dominant Protestant mantra and the most prominent characteristic of the Protestant phronema."
  • Crucial distinction: Scripture alone and Scripture apart — apart from Church, authority, and traditional interpretation.
  • Concedes this is "not the meaning of sola scriptura, but that is its effect."
  • Argument from disagreement: Scripture cannot be self-interpretive, since Protestants themselves do not agree on its meaning.
  • Were it self-interpretive, "everyone would arrive at the same conclusions and interpretations."
  • Result: "an astonishing variety of Protestant denominations and independent congregations."

The Reformation's antitraditionalism (p. 38)

  • The Reformation revolved around rejecting Catholic traditions denounced as errors and deviations from early Church practice.
  • Reformers championed "the Scriptures alone"; the Catholic Church answered with "Scripture and Tradition."
  • Because Catholics insisted on Tradition's authority, Tradition itself came to be rejected wholesale in the Protestant world.
  • Pelikan's irony, quoted: "the very antitraditionalism of the Reformation has itself become a tradition."

Protestant Legalism (p. 38–39)

  • "A subtle spirit of legalism prevails in many Protestant circles in terms of expected behaviors and beliefs."
  • Specific required actions: being sorry for one's sins, accepting Jesus as personal Savior, inviting Him into your heart, saying the sinner's prayer.
  • For some, confessing with the mouth and believing in the heart (Rom. 10:9); for others, laying on of hands or baptism.
  • "A certain rigidity applies even though the requirements for each group differ."
  • The word justification carries legalistic overtones absent from the Greek dikaiosyne.
  • Atonement on the cross is treated as "the sole explanation of salvation," requiring nothing from the believer but assent.
  • That framework "minimizes or ignores the ongoing effects or consequences of sin."
  • Many Protestants still struggle with personal sin and have no theological construct to explain why.
  • They lack a category distinguishing Christ's redemptive death for all from each person's need to respond and "put on Christ" (Gal. 3:27).
  • Without sacramental grace, they cannot actualize the victory of Christ personally.
  • What is offered instead is often "a mere emotional response to Christ" or "a pumped-up gospel of positive thinking and prosperity."
  • Conclusion: "there is no single Protestant phronema," but infinite varieties produced by sola scriptura.
  • Disagreement with a pastor is easily solved by switching denominations or founding one's own congregation.
  • Gallup data cited: US adults identifying with a specific Protestant denomination fell from 50% (2000) to 30% (2016).

The Roman Catholic Phronema (p. 40)

  • Catholic opinion ranges "from extremely liberal to very conservative," making the phronema hard to state conclusively.
  • For this reason she relies on official Vatican documents, canons, and the official Catechism.
  • Announces the four aspects: Roman centrality, faith and reason, development of doctrine, and legalism.

First Aspect: Roman Centrality (p. 40–43)

  • "For Catholics, unity of faith is demonstrated by and through union with Rome."
  • The pope is the visible symbol of union, "the vicar of Christ on earth."
  • Conformity to papal decrees is "often considered the mark of a devout Catholic and is literally demanded in some Vatican documents."
  • The Imprimatur system licensed Catholic publishing; nihil obstat ("nothing hinders") appeared on title pages.
  • Certain categories of book — Bible translations, prayer books, liturgical books, catechisms, theological textbooks — still require approval.
  • Roman centrality has been promoted "at least since the time of Gregory the Great (d. 604)."
  • Catholic theologians turn to Rome's statements to define, affirm, reform, or dispute teaching.
  • Vatican documents function for Catholics the way the Fathers function for Orthodox — her own repeated classroom observation ("There is a document…").
  • Scripture and the Fathers are genuinely regarded as authorities, but quoted in brief excerpts supporting Vatican documents.
  • The Roman documents "become the lens through which the ancient sources are understood and applied."
  • Lumen Gentium 25 requires "loyal submission of the will and intellect" even to non-ex cathedra papal teaching.
  • Footnote detail: the Vatican website's newer translation softens "submission" to "religious assent."
  • The Catholic Church has formal rules governing dissent — when one is "in dissent," what may be dissented from, and the required state of mind.
  • Despite Peter as "the Rock" and a claimed unshakable bastion of truth, the Church "has undergone momentous change over the centuries."

Second Aspect: Faith and Reason (p. 43)

  • Augustine (d. 430) laid the foundation; his writings "so dominated the Latin Church that they fundamentally changed Western thought."
  • He was trained as a philosopher and rhetorician, and "lacking a formal theological education, Augustine relied on his philosophical training."
  • His premise: truth cannot conflict with reason because God is the source of both.
  • Hence deductive reasoning "can be utilized in the service of theology."
  • His method: pose a question, analyze point by point and possibility by possibility, reach a rational conclusion, move on.
  • He did use Scripture and Tradition, but "a theological style based almost exclusively on deductive reasoning had not been used in the East."
  • Relying on philosophy, he reached divergent conclusions on questions "already been resolved by important church councils and notable Greek Fathers."

From Augustine to Anselm (p. 44 — paraphrase)

  • His theological innovations spread widely in the West and contribute to the separation between the churches to this day.
  • Even where his deviations looked philosophically logical or linguistically minor, they had serious lasting consequences.
  • His enormous Latin output made him "the theological authority par excellence" in the West, with unequaled sustained influence.
  • Augustine himself became the standard of orthodoxy for the Latins, producing a new, Latin Tradition.
  • Later Latin Christians leaned on him even more heavily after the decline of the Western Roman Empire.
  • The Greek Fathers had not deviated from apostolic Tradition, but were read far less frequently, if at all, in the West.
  • The shift from apostolic Tradition to reason as theology's source is what made the Western phronema diverge.
  • Anselm of Canterbury (d. 1109) defined theology as "faith seeking understanding," reinforcing logic and deduction.
  • Important qualifier: the Catholic Church does not believe in reason alone and expressly rejects rationalism, biblicism, fideism, and radical traditionalism.
  • Yet Catholics find it impossible to theologize without deductive reasoning — a trait shared by virtually all Western Christians.
  • Protestants inherited this reliance subconsciously, since the phronema predated the Reformation.

What reason claims to reach (p. 45)

  • Catholics do not hold that all mysteries can be understood by reason, and affirm everything begins with revelation.
  • "However, the pervasive emphasis is on the capacity of human reason to arrive at knowledge of God."
  • Orthodox agree creation reveals God's existence — "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Ps. 19:1), and Rom. 1:20.
  • But the Catholic claim goes further: actual knowledge of God attainable through human deductive reasoning.
  • The consequence she draws: if reason is how one knows God, then unbelief or wrong belief is a defect of reason, not of soul.
  • "If one applies the correct reasoning, one will arrive at the correct theological beliefs."

Aquinas and Palamas (p. 46)

  • "The theological methodology initiated by Augustine and strengthened by Anselm was confirmed and cemented permanently in Western Christianity by Thomas Aquinas (d. 1274)."
  • "Aquinas valued the Scriptures and apostolic Tradition and often referred to them."
  • "But by his time, the use of deductive reasoning in theology was firmly established in the Latin Church."
  • He "relied on Aristotelian philosophy and deductive reasoning, and he especially labored to prove that reason did not contradict his theology."
  • "In stark contrast, St. Gregory Palamas, Archbishop of Thessalonika (1347-1359), who flourished not long after Aquinas," took the opposite view.
  • Palamas judged pagan philosophy "overly centered on the activity of the rational aspect of the mind (based principally on discursive thinking, syllogism and so forth)."
  • "The foundational use of philosophy requires theological claims to have a nontheological justification."
  • Schneider: "This view makes theology dependent on philosophy and regards human reason as the ultimate universal arbiter."
  • "Theology in the East is based not on discursive thought but on 'knowledge that stems from the vision of God.'"
  • Summary contrast: "The Western approach is fundamentally intellectual; the Eastern is fundamentally spiritual."
  • "By the Middle Ages a clear divide in phronema was evident."
  • Catholic medieval theology was marked by "Yoking reason to theology, developing logical syllogisms, and addressing theological matters through a progressive series of questions and answers."
  • Footnote: that question-and-answer device is hypophora, and from an Eastern perspective it "proves' nothing, since the truth of the conclusion is highly dependent on the validity of the underlying premises."
  • "The result was a massive and permanent imprint on the Catholic phronema and theological method."
  • "Today Catholic seminarians receive years of training in philosophy."
  • John Paul II's Fides et Ratio emphasized philosophy "as an indispensable foundation for theological study."

Third Aspect: Development of Doctrine (p. 47)

  • Rome's esteem for reason feeds "the Catholic impetus to delineate, define, and explain almost everything theological."
  • Divine truth "was declared once and for all in the mystery of Jesus" but "immersed therefore in time and history."
  • Catholic teaching: with time, theological truth can be better understood and expressed.
  • Doctrine develops because "as the centuries succeed one another, the Church constantly progresses towards the fullness of divine truth."
  • The Orthodox view: the fullness of divine revelation was given once, at Pentecost.
  • Orthodoxy accepts growth in understanding and better articulation over time.
  • But it holds the Church "already possesses the fullness of the truth," not that it is progressing toward it.
  • Therefore Orthodoxy rejects new doctrines and the idea of new or greater revelation.
  • Grammatical argument in the footnote: John 16:13's "will lead" is a "future of one moment," not a continuous future — so the Spirit revealed all truth at Pentecost.

How doctrine actually developed (p. 48–49)

  • Catholic manuals from the Middle Ages to the mid-twentieth century argued systematically from Bible, Fathers, church documents, and logical deduction.
  • Deductive reasoning as proof of doctrine became popular in the Middle Ages.
  • The rationale: Potuit, decuit, fecit — He could, it was fitting, therefore He did.
  • The clearest fruit of this rationale is certain doctrines about Mary.
  • Because Catholicism took over Augustine's view of original sin as inherited guilt and a taint on the soul…
  • …theologians reasoned Mary should not have been born with original sin, being mother of the incarnate Son.
  • God could protect her; He should; therefore He did — and the Immaculate Conception was eventually declared dogma.
  • Hierarchs and theologians then "searched for patristic statements (which they misinterpreted or took out of context) to justify this new dogma."
  • The dogma "evolved entirely because of rational deduction, not because the Fathers imagined it, taught it, or believed it."
  • Next logical step: if she had no sin, she must not have died, since death is sin's consequence.
  • "Many Catholics, including leading theologians, insist that Mary never died."
  • Footnote counter: Dormition ("falling asleep") is the term used for death by Christ and the apostles (John 11:11; 1 Thess. 4:13; 1 Cor. 15:6).
  • Pius XII declared the Bodily Assumption dogma in 1950 while refraining from stating whether she died.
  • He noted explicitly that the push to declare it followed directly from the Immaculate Conception.
  • Papal infallibility is named as another traceable case of creeping progression until dogmatic pronouncement.
  • Newman championed development of doctrine, claiming the apostles left seeds that would naturally grow.
  • Newman "rarely… mention[ed] the necessity of prayer or a profound relationship with God as contributing to theological insight."
  • Fides et Ratio: revelation "stirs the human mind to ceaseless effort… leaving no stone unturned."

Fourth Aspect: Legalism (p. 50)

  • The Catholic Church "operates in a highly structured fashion and expresses itself with the language of law."
  • Saints are canonized "after a lengthy and technical legal process."
  • Pronouncements routinely emphasize regulation, conformity to law, and submission — "quite startling to an Orthodox Christian."
  • "A juridical mentality is foreign to the Orthodox phronema."
  • Orthodox say "Holy Canons," not "Code of Canon Law."
  • Orthodox canons "are not codified, systematized, organized, or updated" and do not function as law.

Sin as Debt (p. 50–51)

  • Legalism in the Catholic view of sin also traces to Augustine.
  • He concluded all human beings "not only inherit mortality and the inclination to sin" from Adam "but they are guilty and legally liable before God" for Adam's sin.
  • Augustine distinguished the Eastern view (inclination to sin after the Fall) from inherited legal liability of guilt.
  • Because Protestantism emerged from the Catholic Church, Protestants share the Catholic conception of sin.
  • Western emphasis on Christ paying the "debt" owed the Father makes rejection of Church and sacraments unsurprising.
  • Bowyer, quoted: atonement theology makes the Church "the means of legal justification which pronounces 'not guilty' through the sacraments, rather than a process which restores the innate goodness of man."
  • McLelland: Roman and Reformed argued the same problems and never questioned the shared agenda.
  • The East never experienced the Reformation's "tension of rival authorities" or "conflicting theologies of grace and sacraments."
  • Guilt was the central starting point in the West; in the East the focal point was "not human sin but human destiny: to become godlike."
  • Negative framing yields "redemption and justification"; the "positive dimension is that of sanctification or deification."
  • Recent Catholic theology has tried to recover the illness language, but piety still preserves the legalistic understanding.

Merit, indulgences, purgatory (p. 52–53)

  • Merit means benefiting from the good deeds of the saints, Mary, or Christ to satisfy the sinner's debt.
  • Bluntly put: "Christ and the saints have a reward owed to them by God, and we can tap into their accomplishments."
  • This mentality is "totally absent from the Orthodox, who consider the saints exemplars and intercessors only."
  • Orthodoxy never uses merit and has no concept of a treasury of stored benefits transferable between persons.
  • "Each person will be judged, and each will stand or fall, on the basis of his or her life alone."
  • Grace is received from God, but grace is not merit.
  • Indulgences are possible because of the Catholic concepts of sin and purgatory.
  • Core teaching: "the guilt of the sin may be forgiven but not the punishment that sin requires."
  • Indulgentiarum Doctrina: punishments are expiated on earth through sorrows and death, "or else in the life beyond through fire and torments."
  • Their stated purposes: purification of souls, defense of the moral order, and "restoration of the glory of God to its full majesty."
  • All who walk the earth "daily commit at least venial sins," so all need mercy from sin's penal consequences.
  • Canon 992 defines an indulgence as remission of temporal punishment for already-forgiven guilt, drawn from "the treasury of the satisfactions of Christ and the saints."
  • Canon 994 allows one to gain indulgences "for oneself or apply them to the dead by way of suffrage."
  • Pope Francis's 2015 Jubilee Indulgence explicitly stated it "can also be obtained for the deceased."
  • Purgatory is presented as "a combination of Roman Catholic legalism and the development of doctrine."

The penance story and the Orthodox alternative (p. 54–56)

  • Personal narrative: at fifteen her family moved to San Diego and enrolled her in a Catholic high school.
  • She had never met a nun or been to Mass; ordinary statements and behaviors surprised her constantly.
  • A borrowed library magazine's cover, held by a single staple, detached while in her possession.
  • She apologized to the elderly librarian nun in full black habit — "The cover fell off."
  • The nun replied, "Say ten Hail Marys and one Our Father."
  • She walked away bewildered; days passed before she realized she had been given a penance.
  • Terms like satisfaction, merit, and atonement "fit into a view of salvation that is highly juridical."
  • These ideas "often seem too legalistic even to Catholics," but the concept has a thousand-year history.
  • It is deeply ingrained in Protestantism too — "paying the price" for sin, being "washed by the blood."
  • Orthodox do believe Christ died for our sins and that His death was redemptive.
  • But that is "not the only way the Scripture speaks of the death of Christ," nor what the early Church emphasized.
  • Christ died for the life of the world (John 3:16), not to pay a debt to the Father.
  • Not because God's majesty was diminished, and "certainly not because an imbalance occurred in the universe" correctable only by blood.
  • Those are "distortions and exaggerations rooted in medieval thought."
  • "Christ sacrificed Himself out of love, not out of necessity."
  • Where Orthodoxy speaks of the "blood," it means union with Christ, sharing His life through the Body and Blood.
  • The early Church "repeatedly emphasized the Incarnation of the Son as far more consequential than the Crucifixion."
  • By uniting human and divine in His Person, Christ sanctified human nature and made union with God possible again.
  • After the cross came the Resurrection; "the cross is never separated from the Resurrection."
  • Orthodox teaching on sin: it distorts the relationship between person and God and between human beings.
  • It is impossible to imagine any human act, however heinous, diminishing God's glory or majesty.
  • Sacramental forgiveness in Orthodoxy "is always complete and immediate," with no penalty outstanding.

Chapter Synthesis

This chapter answers the question Ch. 2 left open: if phronema is real, why do sincere Christians reading the same Bible end up so far apart? Constantinou's answer is that Scripture never interprets itself, so the operative variable is always the mind brought to it. She then reconstructs two Western minds and shows they are one mind with two settings.

The Protestant setting is sola scriptura, which she reads not as "Scripture alone" but "Scripture apart" — cut off from Church and traditional interpretation — with the unavoidable practical result of "myself alone" and endless fragmentation. The Catholic setting is faith and reason, traced to Augustine, a trained philosopher without theological formation who concluded that deduction could serve theology. That single methodological choice, amplified by Anselm and cemented by Aquinas, is what she identifies as the true origin of the schism: not a dispute over a clause, but a change in what theology is for.

Her most compelling section is the worked example. Potuit, decuit, fecit shows a doctrine being generated by pure inference — God could preserve Mary from original sin, it was fitting, therefore He did — after which patristic support was retrofitted, and the next inference followed that Mary never died. This is development of doctrine caught in the act, and it depends entirely on Augustine's prior legal reading of original sin as inherited guilt.

Everything in the fourth section then falls out of that legal premise: if sin is debt, guilt can be forgiven while penalty remains, which requires purgatory, which permits indulgences, which needs a transferable treasury of merit, which produces a nun assigning ten Hail Marys for a detached magazine cover. Against all of it she sets the medical alternative — sin as illness, salvation as healing, the Incarnation weightier than the Crucifixion, forgiveness complete and immediate. The two systems are coherent internally and incommensurable with each other, which is exactly what a difference of phronema, rather than of doctrine, would predict.
