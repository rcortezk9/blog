Text Analysis: Thinking Orthodox, Ch. 6 — Theology and the Theologian

Scan note: Built 2026-08-26 in the two-tier format; this is the first note for Ch. 6. The whole chapter (pp. 107–122) is now clean and quoted verbatim. Pages 108, 110, 112, 116, 118, 120, and 122 had been cropped in the earlier photo scans and were paraphrase-only — this covered the definition of a theologian, the Maximos and Kronstadt citations, St. John's later life, St. Gregory's opponents and the "middle way," St. Symeon's early life and exile, and the closing Ware/Palamas material. All were re-shot with vFlat on 2026-08-26, so that material is now quoted directly.

Curated Takeaways

Core Claims

  • "The word theology (theologia) literally means 'talking or teaching about God.' It also denotes a 'mystical knowledge' of God." — Opens by refusing to let theology mean only the academic discipline; the mystical sense is present in the word itself.
  • "the source of true Christian theology is the confession of the Incarnation of the Son of God." — Lossky's formula, which grounds the chapter: theology is possible at all only because the divine and human were united in Christ.
  • "Recollection that the true theologian is one who prays undermines confidence in oneself and reliance on one's own thoughts and conclusions." — Explains the function of the famous proverb — it is not a definition so much as a brake on the theologian's ego.
  • "it acts as a check against two great enemies of authentic theology: pride and innovation." — Names the chapter's two antagonists, both of which the three exemplar saints are shown resisting.
  • "Emphasis on the intellect and reason is what gives Western Christian theology a more secular and scholastic character. But not all truth conforms to human reason or is verifiable by historical proof or scientific analysis." — Restates the book's thesis as a claim about what theology can and cannot be tested by.
  • "Theology is nothing other than contemplation, theoria, of vision of God. In other words, it is communion with God." — The chapter's culminating definition, which makes the saints rather than the scholars the true theologians.

Supporting Evidence

  • Source: Metropolitan Hierotheos Vlachos, The Mind of the Orthodox Church
    • "We divide people into theologians and non-theologians. We consider that theologians are those who possess some intellectual knowledge about God… we must say that theology is chiefly life and experience, and that theologians, according to the teaching of the Church, are essentially those who see God." — The clearest statement of the redefinition the whole chapter argues for.
  • Source: St. John Chrysostom, Commentary on St. John, Homily 2
    • "it is plain that no part of the teaching… is human, but divine and heavenly are the instructions which have come to us through this divine soul." — Patristic warrant for treating the Fourth Gospel as revealed rather than composed.
  • Source: St. Gregory Palamas, Homily 44.3
    • John "alone, not only of the apostles but of all eminent men before and after him, was called 'virgin' by everyone, for it seems that he alone kept both soul and body, mind and senses, virginal throughout his life." — Links John's theological depth directly to his purity, which is the chapter's causal claim about how theology is acquired.
  • Source: St. Gregory the Theologian, Orations 20.11, 23.12, 27.5
    • "Although one must think of God constantly, he said, one must not speak of God quickly or readily." — The most compact expression of the restraint the chapter recommends, from the only Father the Church titled "the Theologian."
  • Source: St. Gregory the Theologian on method
    • He opposed "futile and blasphemous argumentation" and preferred "the philosophy of the fishermen" to that of Aristotle. — Shows the East had a self-conscious alternative method, not merely an absence of one.

Key Terminology

  • Theologia / theologeo: "The same word functions as a verb in Greek: 'I theologize' (theologeo), meaning, 'I pronounce words about God.'"
  • Theologos: the Fathers' term for "those who spoke about God," applied to David, the prophets, Moses, and the Evangelists — and used for "one who practices mystical prayer."
  • Theoria: contemplation or vision of God; theology "is nothing other than" this.
  • Metanoia, catharsis, askesis: repentance, purification, and spiritual struggle — what theology requires and other sciences do not.
  • Charisma: any illumination the theologian receives is "a free gift of God's grace."
  • "The New Theologian": a label "probably initially intended to be derogatory and critical" that the Church later embraced.

Synthesis & Application

  • Conclusion: "Saint Symeon actually said nothing new or innovative. He advocated a return to true theology: the theologian as the one who prays."
  • Application: "His enthusiastic insistence on the inner mystical life was not a departure from the Holy Fathers but a restoration and a reminder to the faithful that the heart and essence of Orthodox theology is the spiritual life and intimate communion with God."

Full Chapter Facts

(Organized by the chapter's own sections, in source order. One compact line per fact. All rescanned pages are now verbatim; a few lines drawn from p. 114 remain paraphrase and are tagged inline.)

What Is Theology? (p. 107)

  • Theologia means "talking or teaching about God" and also "a mystical knowledge" of God.
  • The verb form theologeo means "I pronounce words about God" or "I speak about God."
  • One theologizes by teaching about God, speaking about God, speaking to God in prayer, or discussing divine truths.
  • The Incarnation makes possible the union of "limited, mortal, fleshy human beings" with "the incomprehensible, immortal, eternal, spiritual Divine."
  • Lossky: "the source of true Christian theology is the confession of the Incarnation of the Son of God."
  • The Fathers applied theologos to those who spoke about God, to preceding Fathers, and to David, the prophets, Moses, and the Evangelists — especially John.
  • Footnote example: Palamas called Chrysostom "the theologian" when quoting his explanation of transfigured.
  • "The Fathers also used the term 'theologian' for one who practices mystical prayer."
  • "Theology can only be from above, since it is words about God. Theology is offered to the glory of God, not ourselves."
  • "Since it is divine, it can never be based on human reasoning, ideas, speculation, or clever argumentation."
  • "Orthodox theology can never be disconnected from the spiritual life of the theologian or from the life of the Church."
  • "Authentic Orthodox theology is 'liturgical, doxological and mystical.'"

What Is a Theologian? (p. 108)

  • "The classic Orthodox definition of a theologian is well known and frequently repeated in Orthodox circles: 'A true theologian is one who prays,' or 'One who prays is a true theologian.'"
  • Attributed originally to Evagrios of Pontus (d. 399).
  • It "reflects the Orthodox phronema and stands in stark contrast to the Western Christian phronema."
  • That contrast is with a phronema "which strongly emphasizes use of the mind for comprehension of theological truths and rational deduction as a theological method."
  • The proverb resonates because "it perfectly encapsulates the acute awareness that one cannot truly know God through book learning or the application of the intellect."
  • "Human reason fails because growth in knowledge of God is possible only through growth in holiness, and one does not acquire holiness through study but through prayer."
  • "Academic learning and study are important and beneficial in the pursuit of theological truth, but they never substitute for the spiritual life."

Humility versus Self-Reliance (p. 109)

  • The proverb "supplies an important restraint" against forging ahead "enamored with his own thoughts."
  • The genuinely Orthodox theologian pauses to ask: "Am I overstepping my bounds? Am I being faithful to apostolic Tradition?"
  • Further questions: "Are the Fathers in agreement with this view? Is this opinion consistent with the mind of the Fathers, even if they have never spoken on this subject specifically?"
  • And: "Should I defer to another's opinion? Should I pray about this? Should I speak to my spiritual father about this? Should I decline to speak about this topic entirely?"
  • Vlachos: theologians are commonly taken to be "those who possess some intellectual knowledge about God."
  • His correction: "theology is chiefly life and experience," and theologians "are essentially those who see God."
  • The proverb checks "two great enemies of authentic theology: pride and innovation."
  • "In the eyes of the world, self-confidence is commendable and valued."
  • Concession: "Intellectual rigor has an important place in Orthodoxy as well, and we celebrate our God-given ability to reason."
  • Western theology instead encourages confidence in one's conclusions "after a thorough intellectual examination and rational analysis."
  • Its slogans are "faith and reason" and "faith seeking understanding."
  • That emphasis is "what gives Western Christian theology a more secular and scholastic character."

The limits of the intellect (p. 110)

  • God is beyond "human comprehension and 'not an object to be studied,' as St. Maximos the Confessor reminds us."
  • Maximos: "I would even venture to affirm that every thought capable of forming an impression in the intellect is nothing other than an elementary outline, pointing to realities that are beyond it."
  • "Orthodox theology is essentially a spiritual pursuit."
  • "Reliance on the self in spiritual matters is the beginning of one's fall."
  • Important qualifier: treating theology as spiritual "does not mean that it is devoid of an intellectual component, that we entirely reject reason or do not endeavor to accurately express our views."
  • "It only means that theologizing cannot exist apart from the spiritual life."
  • St. John of Kronstadt: "Remember that the intellect is the servant of the heart, which is our life."
  • "if it leads the heart to truth, peace, joy and life, then it fulfills its purpose, it is true."
  • "but if it leads the heart to doubt, disturbance, torment, despondency, darkness, then it does not fulfil its purpose, and is absolutely false—knowledge, falsely so called."
  • "It goes without saying that an Orthodox theologian must have an Orthodox phronema."
  • "Furthermore, a theologian must be well educated in all aspects of the Tradition, rather than being a specialist in only one area."

The Title "Theologian" (p. 110)

  • "Three important saints have been given the term 'theologian' as a title to indicate their unique contribution to the theology of the Church."
  • They are "St. John the Theologian (the Evangelist), St. Gregory the Theologian (of Nazianzus), and St. Symeon the New Theologian."

St. John the Theologian (p. 111)

  • One of the Twelve, part of the inner circle among Christ's followers.
  • Raised in Galilee, son of the fisherman Zebedee; he and his older brother James "left everything to follow Jesus."
  • John was the youngest of the Twelve, "probably still a teenager at that time."
  • Being "enthusiastic and possibly even boisterous by nature," the brothers were nicknamed the "sons of thunder" (Mark 3:17).
  • With Peter they formed a group of three taken along at special moments, such as the Transfiguration (Matt. 17:1–8; Mark 9:2–8; Luke 9:28–35).
  • Footnote adds the raising of Jairus's daughter and Gethsemane.
  • John was "the only one among the Twelve to witness the Lord's Crucifixion and death" (John 19:26–27).
  • He was also "the first male disciple to see the empty tomb following the Resurrection" (John 20:4).
  • Called "the Beloved Disciple" or "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (John 13:23; 19:26; 20:2; 21:7, 20).
  • This does not mean Christ loved the others less, but marks "a unique friendship."
  • Dying on the cross, the Lord "showed His confidence in and respect for John by giving His own mother over to John's care."
  • From that hour "John took her to his own home" (John 19:27).
  • He "lived to extreme old age and never married," caring for the Theotokos and preaching.
  • Palamas: John alone "was called 'virgin' by everyone," keeping "soul and body, mind and senses, virginal throughout his life."
  • Palamas: "Few people practice bodily virginity, but nearly everyone knows what it is, whereas perfect virginity of the soul means keeping the mind free from all association with evil."
  • His conclusion: "this name bears witness to the fact that John was almost sinless, and that is why he came to be beloved of Christ, who alone was sinless by nature."

John's later life (p. 112)

  • "After Pentecost, John went to Asia Minor, preaching the gospel, establishing churches there, and finally settling in Ephesus."
  • "He suffered for Christ many times and in many ways through hardships, beatings, and imprisonments."
  • Persecution began "under the Roman emperor Domitian (who reigned AD 81-96)."
  • "the Christians of Asia Minor faced torture, imprisonment, and martyrdom."
  • "Saint John himself was put into a large vat full of boiling oil, but he survived."
  • "The Romans resorted to exiling John to the island of Patmos, where he wrote the Book of Revelation."
  • There "he encouraged the seven churches of Asia to remain faithful to Christ even if it meant martyrdom."
  • "After the death of Domitian, John's exile ended, and he returned to Ephesus."
  • "John lived to extreme old age and died during the reign of Trajan" (98–117), per Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.3.4, and Eusebius.
  • "Saint John the Theologian's feast day is September 26, or October 9 on the old calendar."
  • A second feast on May 8 (or May 21) commemorates "a unique event that took place every year at his tomb in Ephesus."
  • "A delicate but fragrant dust accumulated on the tomb on that date. The faithful would gather it and use it to heal the sick."

Why John is called "the Theologian" (p. 113)

  • Chrysostom: "it is plain that no part of the teaching… is human, but divine and heavenly are the instructions which have come to us through this divine soul."
  • All four evangelists "understood and demonstrated that the Lord is both human and divine."
  • But John's Gospel "expressed the deepest and most profound truths regarding the divine nature of the Son of God."
  • "The Theologian's Gospel provided the sword of the Spirit that enabled the Church to slay heresy."
  • The Fathers refuted denials of the Son's and Spirit's divinity "by citing verses from the Gospel of John."
  • "The language of the Fourth Gospel is embedded and enshrined in the Nicene Creed."
  • Footnote specifics: Christ as "only-begotten Son of God" (John 1:18) and the Spirit who "proceeds from the Father" (John 15:26).
  • John articulated that the Son always existed, is equal to the Father, begotten not created, and took on human flesh.
  • Such mystical concepts, "incomprehensible to the human mind, were rendered into human words" by one "whose mind had been illuminated by divine grace."

St. Gregory the Theologian — early life (p. 113, 115)

  • Born in 330 at his family's home at Arianzum, near Nazianzus in Cappadocia (now central Turkey).
  • His father, also named Gregory, was a bishop; his mother Nonna "was a woman of deep piety."
  • He was educated in Nazianzus, then Caesarea, then Palestine, then Alexandria, "finally completing his studies in Athens."
  • In Athens he and St. Basil the Great "met and became the closest of friends." (paraphrase)
  • Afterward both lived for a time in the wilderness of Pontus, in prayer, fasting, and study. (paraphrase)
  • Neither would be allowed to remain long in prayer, study, and solitude. (paraphrase)
  • Both were elevated to the priesthood and then the episcopacy. (paraphrase)
  • They matured during the Arian heresy, which denied the divinity of the Son and the Holy Spirit. (paraphrase)
  • Nicaea condemned Arianism in 325, but the heresy "actually grew in strength" afterward, aided by Arian emperors. (paraphrase)
  • Gregory was "quiet [and] introspective by nature," preferring to live far from cities, strife, and controversy. (paraphrase)
  • He valued secular education, "which he was convinced could be useful in the [service] of the Church." (paraphrase)
  • His father ordained him a presbyter "praying all the while that he would not be called to an even higher office."

Gregory ordained bishop (p. 115)

  • Caesarea was capital of the very large province of Cappadocia, administered by Basil as archbishop.
  • The emperor Valens, "an Arian who opposed orthodox Christianity," ruled at the time.
  • Valens opposed Nicaea and opposed Basil, "since Basil was a popular bishop and a vocal supporter of Nicaea."
  • He sought to undermine Basil "by dividing the sizable province of Cappadocia into two provinces."
  • This created "a position for a rival bishop and a haven for Arians."
  • Basil ordained "a reluctant Gregory as bishop of Sasima in 372."
  • Sasima "has been described as not even a village but a mere crossroads," and Gregory spent little time there.
  • Basil died in 379, but first urged fellow bishops "to enlist the help of Gregory to fight the Arian heresy."
  • Gregory went to Constantinople, "where not even a single church remained under orthodox control."
  • He "transformed his residence into a small church," preaching the Five Theological Orations (27–31) on the Persons of the Trinity.
  • He "slowly transformed the city, leading it back to Nicene orthodoxy," and was chosen Archbishop of Constantinople.

Opposition and the Second Council (p. 116)

  • "Through his deep theological insight and oratorical skills, Gregory accurately and persuasively expressed the relationship between the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. It was not easy."
  • "His Arian opponents claimed that Gregory was inciting violence and disturbing the peace with his preaching."
  • "They slandered him, claiming that he was teaching that there are three gods."
  • "Gregory was attacked by thugs who even attempted to murder him."
  • "Stones were hurled at him, and at times violent mobs even descended on the small church and threatened his life."
  • "But Gregory persisted, and he gradually changed hearts and minds."
  • "As archbishop of Constantinople, he presided over the Second Ecumenical Council in 381."
  • "The council completed the Creed, most of which had been written at Nicaea."
  • "The final section was composed at Constantinople, including the line that clearly proclaimed the divinity of the Holy Spirit."
  • That line, quoted in the footnote: "And I believe in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Creator of life, who proceeds from the Father, who together with the Father and Son is worshipped and glorified, who spoke through the prophets."
  • He "employed his oratorical skills, excellent education, brilliant mind, holiness, and deep life of prayer to defeat Arianism and other heresies."
  • Acknowledging that, "the Church gratefully bestowed upon Gregory the title by which he would forever be known: 'the Theologian.'"

Gregory and the "Middle Way" (p. 116–117)

  • "Saint Gregory rejected theological extremes and often promoted the 'middle way,' neither too far to the right nor to the left, in both theology and behavior."
  • "One heretical sect, the Eunomians, maintained that God could be comprehended through the operation of the rational mind alone."
  • "A different heretical sect, the Apollinarians, taught that the human mind was so hopelessly sinful that it could not know God at all."
  • "Both sides took extreme positions, and neither was correct."
  • "Against the Eunomians, Gregory refuted the idea that one can achieve actual knowledge of God simply through the mind and the use of human reason."
  • "But he also opposed the Apollinarians, who rejected actual knowledge of God, because we were created to know God."
  • He also opposed the Apollinarians, because "the whole human person, including the human mind, was made in the image and likeness of God."
  • "Gregory explained that knowledge of God is achieved gradually, and most people never attain it."
  • He "rejected idle curiosity and theological sophistry, strongly opposing 'futile and blasphemous argumentation.'"
  • He warned "against theologizing prematurely and without due preparation."
  • Though he used philosophical terms, he preferred "the philosophy of the fishermen" to that of Aristotle.
  • His account of the work of theology is called an important additional contribution beyond his Trinitarian teaching.
  • "Perhaps more than any other Father of the Church, Gregory openly discussed the difficulties, errors, and pitfalls in theologizing."
  • His framework for theology is "rooted above all in prayer and silence."
  • He "advocated restraint in theologizing and sometimes even refused to theologize… to his own bishops."
  • Key maxim: "one must think of God constantly… [but] one must not speak of God quickly or readily."
  • Knowledge of God comes through the mind "but only with the aid of prayer and ascetic discipline."
  • "Theology is not for all people or for all occasions."
  • Striking claim: "Knowledge of theological matters is not necessary for salvation; only simple faith is needed."
  • "Speaking of God is a great task, but spiritual purification is far more important."

St. Symeon the New Theologian — early life (p. 118)

  • "Saint Symeon the New Theologian was born in Galatia, in Asia Minor, in 949 to upper-middle-class parents whose relatives had connections in the court of the Byzantine emperor."
  • "An uncle recognized Symeon's unique qualities, brought him to Constantinople, advanced his education, and encouraged his ascent in Byzantine society."
  • "Symeon worked at a variety of jobs, including manager of an aristocrat's household."
  • "Eventually he assumed various responsibilities as a minor imperial official, possibly even serving as a diplomat for the emperor."
  • "All the while, however, his deepest desire was to become a monk."
  • "At the age of fourteen the young Symeon had met St. Symeon the Studite at the famous monastery in Constantinople known as the Studion."
  • The elder became his spiritual father "but he forbade the younger Symeon to enter the monastic life before the age of twenty-seven."
  • In those years he "worked during the day at his occupation in the world while he spent his evenings immersed in intense prayer and reading spiritual writings as directed by his spiritual father."
  • "At this early point in his life, even before he had been tonsured a monk, he saw the Uncreated Light, a direct experience of God."
  • Footnote: "we can experience God directly through His 'energies,' which are distinct from the essence of God that can never be known or directly experienced."
  • That distinction "goes back to the Cappadocian Fathers (fourth century) and is essential to the Orthodox understanding of 'deification' or union with God."
  • "Many saints have seen the Uncreated Light, especially through extensive practice of the Jesus Prayer."
  • Palamas defended it "by appealing to the Fathers, the Tradition of the Church, and the genuine experience of the Uncreated Light by saints of the Church in his famous work In Defense of the Holy Hesychasts, composed around 1340."

Symeon's reform (p. 119)

  • At twenty-seven he entered the Monastery of St. Mammas in Constantinople.
  • By thirty-one he "had already been ordained a priest and elected the abbot."
  • "The spiritual health of the monks there was poor."
  • The Byzantine Empire was flourishing "and a secular spirit prevailed."
  • Many monks "had become quite worldly, especially those living in large cities."
  • Monasteries were wealthy, and monastic life "was no longer marked by poverty and detachment."
  • The divine services "had become rigid and formal, with emphasis on the observance of ritualized forms rather than on achieving communion with God."
  • Symeon called for "a return to traditional monasticism with its ascetic practices and deep prayer life."
  • His aim extended beyond monasteries: "all Christians can and should experience the Holy Spirit's activity within them."
  • Merely knowing one possessed the Spirit through baptism and chrismation "was not sufficient."
  • "Every Christian ought to have a genuine experience of God, which is possible through fasting, repentance, and prayer."
  • He knew this firsthand, "since he himself had experienced the Uncreated Light while still a layman living in the world."
  • A "Western, almost scholastic quality was creeping into Orthodox theology at that time."
  • It was "abstract, technical, and philosophically based," and "secular because it was divorced from the spiritual life."
  • Crucially, it was "also rigid, conservative, and therefore considered 'Orthodox' by many."
  • Her key observation: "The content may have been traditional, but the spirit and manner of theologizing at that time were foreign to the Orthodox phronema."

Opposition and exile (p. 120)

  • His critics were among the most powerful, highly educated "men in Byzantine society. They considered themselves to be the guardians of Orthodox theology and tradition."
  • "But their theology had begun to imitate Western Christianity's highly stylized and overly intellectual approach."
  • This displaced "the Orthodox stress on spirituality and true knowledge of God through a life of prayer."
  • "Symeon advocated a true theology: knowledge of God through a robust life of the Spirit rather than a theology of dead formalism."
  • "His message was not well received."
  • "As Symeon pushed the monks of his monastery toward higher levels of holiness and contemplation, most of them failed to appreciate his efforts and reforms."
  • "Quite a few rebelled against him, and on one occasion some monks even attempted to attack him violently."
  • "Symeon had enemies in very high places. The chief theologian advising the emperor was Archbishop Stephen."
  • Stephen fought his ideas, "believing they threatened and undermined ecclesiastical authority in favor of individualistic charismatic experiences."
  • "Symeon also attacked corruption among the priests and bishops at that time, which further alienated Church authorities."
  • Her verdict: he "was undermining neither Holy Tradition nor Church authority but was passionately promoting the primary goal of the Christian life, which was being neglected: personal transformation and intimate communion with God."
  • Speaking openly of mystical experiences was "a shocking thing, especially for a monk, but he did so because he believed that the Holy Spirit compelled him to."
  • "This, no doubt, also undermined his credibility with powerful men in the Church, since it suggested a lack of humility."
  • "Eventually, Symeon was exiled from Constantinople in 1009. He crossed the Bosporus and lived in a small town on the seashore, where he established a humble monastery at the Church of St. Marina."
  • "His exile was later lifted, and he was invited to return to Constantinople, but St. Symeon chose to remain at St. Marina."
  • There he lived "a quiet life, writing and offering spiritual direction until his death in 1022."

A "New" Theologian (p. 121)

  • "It is quite likely that the appellation 'the New Theologian' was not intended as a compliment."
  • "Orthodox Christianity rejects innovation in favor of" maintaining sacred Tradition unchanged.
  • His zeal and enthusiasm were surprising, "and some of his statements could easily be misunderstood."
  • "Saint Symeon actually said nothing new or innovative."
  • He advocated "a return to true theology: the theologian as the one who prays."
  • The title "New Theologian" was "probably initially intended to be derogatory and critical."
  • It survived and "was actually embraced by the Church after his death."
  • This indicates "rather than deviating from Tradition, the saint had effected a return to it."
  • Telling point: "If indeed his teachings had been entirely new, he would not have been regarded as a theologian at all."
  • His most unique contribution is "his candid descriptions of his personal encounters with the living Christ."
  • The experiences themselves "were not unique to Symeon" — many Fathers mention them, "but only in brief hints."
  • Orthodox, "particularly monastics, are taught not to speak of their personal spiritual life and experiences."
  • "Never has any Orthodox saint so deliberately or purposefully attempted to communicate such deep and inexpressible spiritual delights."
  • He endured "sharp criticism, hardship, and even exile," but "was driven by the Holy Spirit, not by his own ego."
  • His work endures "as a reminder of the profound spiritual transformation possible for all Christians."

The Saints as the True Theologians (p. 121–122)

  • "Theology is nothing other than contemplation, theoria, of vision of God. In other words, it is communion with God."
  • This ties theology "to worship, prayer, ascetic practice, and the spiritual life, since that is how one comes to know and encounter God."
  • "Metropolitan Kallistos Ware asks whether anyone can dare to call himself or herself a theologian, considering what this truly means."
  • "But he notes that, in fact, the term is used routinely to describe those who serve the Church as teachers of theology."
  • Palamas "referred to the saints as the true theologians."
  • But "those who trust the experience of the saints can also be called theologians and may even become good theologians, but on a 'secondary' level."
  • "Others, who do not have experience of God nor trust the saints, are bad theologians or actually not theologians at all."
  • "Ultimately, theology is not a set of definitions or theories."
  • "Theology is mystery since it transcends the rational mind and attempts to express the inexpressible."
  • "In schools of theology and seminaries, theology is indeed an academic subject and, as such, it requires accuracy and embraces a certain 'intellectual rigour.'"
  • This does not conflict with Orthodoxy, "since 'we do not serve the Kingdom of God through vagueness, muddle and lazy thinking.'"
  • Decisive contrast: "in other sciences or areas of investigation, the personal sanctity of the scientist or inquirer is irrelevant."
  • "This is not the case with theology, which requires metanoia (repentance), catharsis (purification), and askesis (spiritual struggle)."
  • "any illumination received by the theologian is charisma, a free gift of God's grace."
  • "Since human language is entirely incapable of expressing divine mystery, the theologian cannot entirely conceptualize, understand, or explain these mysteries."
  • He "seeks to convey, as best he or she can, the experience of God as well as His activity in the Church and in the world, for the glory of God and for the benefit of the Church."

Chapter Synthesis

The chapter takes the abstract argument of the first five and asks who is actually authorized to do theology. Its answer is the old proverb — the true theologian is the one who prays — but Constantinou's interest is less in the definition than in what it does. It functions as a brake. The set of questions she puts in the Orthodox theologian's mouth (Am I overstepping? Are the Fathers agreed? Should I decline to speak at all?) is the practical shape of a phronema that treats pride and innovation as the two standing threats.

The three biographies are chosen to make that concrete rather than to decorate it. John's theological depth is traced to purity of soul, not intellect — Palamas's point about his lifelong virginity is doing causal work. Gregory is the reluctant one: pushed into Sasima, pushed into Constantinople, preferring silence, stoned by mobs, and remembered for restraint rather than output. His "middle way" between the Eunomians (reason reaches God entirely) and those who thought the mind too fallen to know God at all is the chapter's balance point, and his sharpest line — that theological knowledge is unnecessary for salvation, where simple faith suffices — quietly demotes the whole enterprise the book is about.

Symeon supplies the most useful case because he shows the failure mode from the inside. Tenth-century Constantinople had theology that was traditional in content yet scholastic in spirit, formal, technical, divorced from prayer — and, being rigid and conservative, was widely taken to be the Orthodox position. That is the chapter's most transferable warning: correct content plus wrong manner still yields a foreign phronema, and the people best placed to miss it are the learned guardians. Symeon was called "New" as an insult, and the fact that the Church kept the title is her proof that what looked like innovation was recovery.

The closing move follows from all three. If theology is theoria, vision of God, then the saints are the theologians and everyone else is working at second hand — legitimately, if they trust the saints' experience; not at all, if they do not. This is the one field, she notes, where the investigator's own sanctity is not incidental to the result.
