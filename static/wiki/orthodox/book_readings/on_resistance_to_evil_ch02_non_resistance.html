"Resist the devil, and he will flee from you."
— James 4:7

Before you read: This chapter redraws a line you may have assumed was fixed. Ilyin is not defending violence here — he is dismantling the idea that "non-resistance" ever meant what it sounds like it means. Read slowly through the middle section on the "spiritual kremlin"; it is the theological engine of the whole chapter, and it is easy to rush past because it reads like psychology before it reveals itself as ascetic theology. Let it land as a description of your own inner walls, not someone else's.

Chapter Overview

Chapter 2 makes a deceptively simple but load-bearing move: it argues that no honest person actually practices "non-resistance to evil" in the literal sense, because total non-resistance — external and internal — would mean the complete surrender of the soul to evil, its transformation into evil's "instrument," "cesspool," and "plaything." Even a person who never lifts a hand or raises their voice against wrongdoing still resists evil internally so long as they feel disgust, condemnation, or resistance of will; total non-resistance would require silencing even that inner revulsion, which is itself already the beginning of consent to evil and, Ilyin argues, the beginning of spiritual and even psychological disintegration. The chapter then pivots to genuine theological substance: it distinguishes negative asceticism (struggle against evil) from positive asceticism (positive absolution/purification), credits pre-Christian and especially Eastern Orthodox ascetic teachers — Anthony the Great, Macarius the Great, Mark the Ascetic, Ephraim the Syrian, and others — with the deepest treatment in history of internal resistance to evil thoughts, and closes by conceding that Tolstoy and his followers do not, in fact, ask for total non-resistance; their actual position is narrower: struggle against evil is necessary, but must be waged only by certain approved (non-forceful) means. This concession is decisive for the whole book — it reframes the entire controversy: the real question is not whether to resist evil, but by what means, which is precisely the question the rest of the book will spend its length answering.

Main Points

1. Total Non-Resistance Is Impossible for an Honest Soul — It Would Mean Total Surrender

Core Argument: Genuine, complete non-resistance — external and internal — is not a moral position but a description of total capitulation: subordination to evil, participation in it, and finally becoming its instrument.

Historical Context: Ilyin is responding to a popular misreading of Tolstoyan "non-resistance" as literal passivity; he wants to clear this misunderstanding away before engaging Tolstoy's actual, more limited position later in the chapter.

Biblical Foundation: James 4:7's command to "resist the devil" assumes resistance is not optional for a soul in right relationship with God — the alternative to resistance is not neutrality but subjection. Ephesians 6:13's call to "withstand in the evil day" carries the same assumption: a soul that does not withstand does not remain neutral, it falls.

Patristic Witness: St. John Cassian, whom Ilyin cites directly, teaches that "no one can be deceived by the devil but one who has chosen to yield to him... the consent of his own will" (Conferences) — the same logic Ilyin uses: surrender is always, at bottom, a choice, even when it is disguised as passivity.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Even someone "quite internally... silent" who never externally acts is "already engaged in internal resistance" as long as any dislike or disgust toward evil persists in the soul.
  • B. The person who suppresses even that inner disgust must first "assure himself that evil is not so bad" — the pathway to total surrender runs through self-deception about evil's nature, not through a single dramatic capitulation.
  • C. Once aversion fully subsides, "the soul begins to believe that black is white" — moral perception itself degrades, not merely moral action.

Practical Application: The presence of inner discomfort, guilt, or disgust when we witness or participate in wrongdoing is not spiritual failure to be smoothed over — it is evidence that resistance, however faint, is still alive in the soul, and should be protected rather than silenced.

Catechumenate Note: For a catechumen learning to examine conscience, this chapter offers real comfort: the felt tension of "I know this is wrong but I still find myself drawn to it" is not proof of hypocrisy or failure — it is the very sign that the soul has not yet surrendered. The absence of that tension would be the more dangerous state.

2. Evil Is a Real Spiritual Force Within the Soul, Not an Abstraction

Core Argument: Evil is not "an empty word... a mere logical possibility... a result of subjective evaluation" but a genuine spiritual inclination inherent in every person, "a passionate desire to unbridle the beast inside."

Historical Context: This directly opposes Enlightenment-influenced moral relativism (evil as merely a social construction or subjective judgment) that had gained ground among segments of the Russian intelligentsia Ilyin already criticized in Chapter 1.

Biblical Foundation: Romans 7:21–23's description of "another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind" gives the classic scriptural articulation of evil as an internal, active force rather than a mere idea — Paul does not describe sin as an opinion but as something that "dwells within me" (Romans 7:17).

Patristic Witness: The entire logismoi (thoughts) tradition of the desert Fathers — most systematically articulated by Evagrius Ponticus and inherited throughout Eastern monasticism — treats evil thoughts as quasi-personal assailants requiring active, ongoing combat, not as neutral mental content to be reasoned away.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Evil "seeks to seep through" the obstacles of conscience, shame, and justice — described with the language of active siege, not passive presence.
  • B. Ilyin names this force as attempting to "topple and rend asunder" the "strong-willed walls of the individual kremlin" — a martial, defensive image of the soul under attack.
  • C. This force is present "in each of us" — Ilyin does not exempt the pious or the strong from this reality; it is a universal feature of the human condition.

Practical Application: Treating temptation and evil impulse as requiring active, vigilant resistance — rather than passive tolerance on the theory that "it's just a thought" — is not paranoia but realism about the soul's actual condition.

Catechumenate Note: This is the theological ground for the practice of watchfulness (νῆψις) a catechumen will be introduced to: evil thoughts are engaged and refused at the threshold, not entertained on the assumption that thoughts are morally inert until acted upon.

3. The "Spiritual Kremlin": Virtue as Fortification

Core Argument: The soul's moral and spiritual life depends on actively built and maintained inner walls — shame, duty, conscience, sense of justice, love of God and homeland — and spiritual education is precisely the work of constructing, maintaining, and defending these walls.

Historical Context: The image of the "kremlin" (fortress) is a deliberately Russian one — evoking the literal fortified citadels at the heart of Russian cities — mapped onto the architecture of the soul, giving Ilyin's ascetic psychology a distinctly national and civilizational resonance.

Biblical Foundation: Proverbs 25:28 — "A man without self-control is like a city broken into and left without walls" — is the direct scriptural ancestor of Ilyin's image; the soul's defenses are civic and architectural metaphors throughout wisdom literature.

Patristic Witness: St. Mark the Ascetic's teaching that vigilance (τήρησις) over the heart is the precondition for all other virtue mirrors Ilyin's claim that these walls must be actively "built, maintained, and defended," not simply inherited or assumed.

Sub-Points:

  • A. The walls are built from concrete spiritual materials: "the sense of shame, the sense of duty, the living impulses of conscience and the sense of justice, the need for beauty."
  • B. When these walls soften or disintegrate, the result is described as "the spiritual end of the personality" — not merely moral decline but a kind of ontological dissolution of the self into "chaotically permissive" formlessness.
  • C. The less a person resists evil, the closer they move toward this dissolved state — resistance and personal integrity are causally linked, not merely correlated.

Practical Application: Habits of conscience, shame at wrongdoing, and a felt sense of duty are not psychological baggage to be therapeutically dissolved but load-bearing structures of a stable, free personality — their erosion should be treated as a serious spiritual event, not a liberation.

Catechumenate Note: This gives theological weight to the catechumenate's emphasis on forming conscience before baptism — the walls Ilyin describes are precisely what catechetical formation exists to help construct in a soul entering the Church, especially one coming from a culture actively working to dissolve shame, duty, and conscience as categories.

4. The Total Non-Resistor Becomes Possessed — And Ilyin Concedes Tolstoy Never Actually Argued This

Core Argument: A person who genuinely ceases all resistance, internal and external, becomes "absorbed" by evil and "possessed," ending in psychic or bodily ruin — but Ilyin explicitly concedes that Tolstoy himself never advocated this total non-resistance; his real position restricts the means of resistance, not resistance itself.

Historical Context: This concession is the chapter's hinge. Having spent most of the chapter describing the horror of total non-resistance, Ilyin turns and admits Tolstoy's actual doctrine is narrower and more defensible than the caricature — a mark of intellectual honesty that sharpens rather than weakens his eventual critique.

Biblical Foundation: The Gospels' own accounts of demonic possession (e.g., Mark 5:1–13, the Gerasene demoniac) provide the imaginative and theological backdrop against which Ilyin's language of "possession" and being "absorbed" by evil is not merely metaphorical flourish but a claimed continuity with the Church's understanding of what happens when a soul is wholly given over.

Patristic Witness: St. Ephraim the Syrian and the ascetic Fathers Ilyin names — Anthony the Great, Macarius the Great, Mark the Ascetic — are credited here explicitly as having developed, with unmatched "depth and wisdom," the theology of "tireless inner battle with unseen... attached evil thoughts," which Ilyin treats as the true Christian answer to the whole non-resistance question, prior to and deeper than the political question of physical force.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin distinguishes pre-Christian Eastern asceticism's two inclinations — negative (struggle) and positive (absolution/purification) — situating Christian ascetic practice as a fulfillment rather than an invention.
  • B. He invokes St. Paul's "not the weapons of the world" (a clear echo of 2 Corinthians 10:4, "the weapons of our warfare are not carnal") to frame inner spiritual combat as the deepest and most universally agreed-upon form of "resistance to evil."
  • C. Having established this, Ilyin states plainly: Tolstoy's non-resistance "means resistance and struggle... only by certain favored means" — the disagreement is a disagreement about ways and means, not about whether the struggle against evil is obligatory.

Practical Application: This reframing should discipline how believers argue about pacifism, self-defense, and the use of force generally — the productive question is almost never "should evil be resisted" but "by what means, in what circumstances, at what cost to the soul."

Catechumenate Note: A catechumen should take from this chapter that Orthodoxy's deepest and most developed answer to evil is the ascetic one — inner combat against evil thoughts — and that any conversation about physical force or civic resistance (the subject of later chapters) sits downstream of, and must never substitute for, this prior and more fundamental inner struggle.

Bible Verse Deep Dives

James 4:7

  • Context: Part of James's exhortation to humility and submission to God as the ground of spiritual victory: "Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you."
  • Theological Significance: Resistance to evil is commanded, not optional, and is explicitly paired with submission to God — resistance to evil and submission to God are two faces of one act, not competing postures.
  • Use in Chapter: Provides the direct scriptural refutation of any doctrine of literal non-resistance; James assumes active resistance is simply what a faithful soul does.
  • Cross-References: Ephesians 6:11–13; 1 Peter 5:8–9; Romans 7:21–23.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Romans 7:21–23

  • Context: Paul's description of the divided self — "the good that I would I do not... but the evil which I would not, that I do" — a searing first-person account of sin as internal force, not mere external act.
  • Theological Significance: Grounds Ilyin's claim that evil is "the spiritual inclination of man inherent in each of us" in Paul's own anthropology of indwelling sin at war with the mind.
  • Use in Chapter: Underwrites the chapter's central claim that evil is a real internal force requiring active combat, not a neutral abstraction.
  • Cross-References: Galatians 5:17; James 1:14–15; Romans 8:5–8.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Orthodox Lens

Liturgical Connection: The prayers of the Compline and Midnight Office ("O Heavenly King," the prayers against night terrors and evil thoughts) presuppose exactly the picture Ilyin paints: the soul is understood as needing continual, active defense against besieging evil, especially in stillness — the same "kremlin" logic given liturgical voice.

Ascetic Formation: This chapter is, functionally, a condensed introduction to the logismoi tradition. Ilyin's image of walls "built, maintained, and defended" against a besieging force is the ascetic theology of watchfulness (νῆψις) translated into political-philosophical language — the same discipline the Philokalia describes as guarding the heart (τήρησις καρδίας) is what Ilyin calls maintaining the "spiritual kremlin."

Sacramental Theology: The chapter's account of the "non-resistor" who talks himself into believing "evil is not so bad" describes, in reverse, exactly the disposition Confession exists to interrupt — Confession is the sacramental mechanism by which a softening conscience is re-hardened, walls that have been breached are rebuilt, before the "black is white" inversion Ilyin describes becomes total.

Patristic Harmony: The named Fathers — Anthony the Great, Macarius the Great, Mark the Ascetic, Ephraim the Syrian, and (per the likely mistranslation "John of the latter") John Climacus, author of The Ladder of Divine Ascent — together represent the classical Egyptian-Syrian-Sinaite axis of the ascetic tradition on inner warfare. Ilyin's claim that "nowhere is this internal resistance to evil developed with such depth and wisdom" as among these teachers is a substantive historical judgment, not mere piety — the Philokalic tradition really is the most sustained body of literature in any tradition on the phenomenology of resisting evil thought at its root.

Thematic Concept Analysis

1. Resistance as a Precondition of Selfhood
Definition in Orthodox context: the soul's coherence and freedom depend on its ongoing resistance to evil; total non-resistance is not a diminished self but the dissolution of the self.
Development through the chapter: the "spiritual kremlin" image, the language of walls softening into "chaotically permissive" formlessness.
Soteriological/eschatological implications: ties directly to theosis — if evil unresisted means dissolution of personhood, then resistance to evil is not merely defensive but constitutive of the very personhood that is being progressively deified.

2. Evil as Active Force, Not Passive Absence
Definition: evil is not simply the privation of good in a static sense but an active, encroaching spiritual reality that "seeks to seep through" defenses.
Development: the besieging, martial language throughout — evil "spreads its wings," "mustered its forces" (Ch. 1), "seeks to seep through," "topples and rends asunder" (Ch. 2).
Implications: while classical Christian metaphysics (following Augustine and, differently, the Cappadocians) defines evil as privatio boni (absence of good), Ilyin's phenomenological description does not contradict this — an absence can still be experienced and must still be actively resisted, the way a collapsing wall is a real event even though the collapse itself is only the absence of structural integrity.

3. Consent as the Hinge of Spiritual Catastrophe
Definition: no soul is overtaken by evil without its own will's consent somewhere in the process — possession, in Ilyin's account, is always downstream of chosen self-deception.
Development: the sequence from disgust, to rationalizing evil as "not so bad," to belief that "black is white," to total absorption.
Implications: preserves human freedom and responsibility even in describing the most extreme spiritual collapse — this is not deterministic corruption but a chosen, if often self-obscured, surrender, echoing Cassian's dictum that no one is deceived except by their own will's consent.

4. The Reframing of the "Non-Resistance" Debate
Definition: the realization that the real dispute is never "resist or not" but "by what means" — a methodological pivot that restructures the entire remaining argument of the book.
Development: Ilyin's explicit concession at the chapter's close that Tolstoy does call for struggle, only by restricted means.
Implications: models intellectual charity even toward an opponent Ilyin will spend the rest of the book arguing against — correctly identifying the actual point of disagreement before attacking it.

Reflection Questions

  1. (Comprehension) According to Ilyin, why is it impossible for an honest person to practice total, literal non-resistance to evil?
  2. (Comprehension) What concession does Ilyin make about Tolstoy's actual position at the end of the chapter, and how does it change the nature of the disagreement between them?
  3. (Theological/Analytical) Ilyin describes evil as an active, besieging force rather than a mere absence of good. How does this square with, or diverge from, the classical Christian doctrine of evil as privatio boni?
  4. (Theological/Analytical) Cassian's teaching that "no one can be deceived by the devil but one who has chosen to yield to him" places responsibility for spiritual catastrophe squarely on the will. Does this adequately account for cases of severe trauma, manipulation, or diminished capacity — or does it need qualification?
  5. (Personal/Devotional) Where in your own life have you noticed the early stages of the pattern Ilyin describes — inner discomfort with something, followed by the temptation to tell yourself "it's not so bad"? What stopped, or didn't stop, the process?
  6. (Personal/Devotional) What are the specific "walls" of your own spiritual kremlin — the concrete habits of conscience, shame, or duty that you rely on? Which feel strong right now, and which feel like they need rebuilding?
  7. (Liturgical/Sacramental) How does the practice of Confession function, in light of this chapter, as a means of "rebuilding the walls" once they have been breached by rationalized sin?
  8. (Liturgical/Sacramental) The Compline and Midnight prayers ask for protection against evil especially in stillness and darkness. How does this chapter's picture of evil "seeking to seep through" our defenses deepen your understanding of why the Church prays this way at those specific times?
