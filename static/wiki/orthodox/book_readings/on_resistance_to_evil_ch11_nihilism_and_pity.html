#asceticism#book/on_resistance_to_evil_by_force#book_study#moral_theology#orthodox#theology

"We glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; and patience, experience; and experience, hope."
— Romans 5:3-4

Before you read: This chapter widens Ilyin's critique from love to the whole of spiritual culture, and then delivers what may be the book's single most countercultural claim: suffering is not evil, and the sentimental moralist's foundational axiom — that it is — is simply false. Ilyin argues that treating the avoidance of suffering as the highest good does not just distort the resistance question; it devastates religion, science, art, law, and the state along the way. Read the middle section on suffering carefully; it is not a defense of cruelty but an argument that the "way up" for the human soul runs, unavoidably, through hardship it cannot bypass.

Chapter Overview

Ilyin argues that Tolstoy's sentimental love is not merely weak-willed and egocentric (as Chapter 10 showed) but actively anti-spiritual, and this shows up as a cascading nihilism across every domain of culture. Moral experience, elevated above all else, judges and devalues religion (dismissing dogma and mystery as incomprehensible to "common sense," producing religious nihilism), science (reducing truth to moral utility, producing scientific nihilism), art (demanding moral usefulness of every work, producing aesthetic nihilism), and law and the state (seeing only "harsh violence" in courts, punishment, and the sword, and rejecting property, military service, and national defense entirely, producing juridical-state-patriotic nihilism). What survives this total devastation is a person reduced to a single dimension: a suffering or pitying "moral atom," stripped of religion, homeland, art, and law. Ilyin then unearths the hidden axiom beneath all of this: sentimental morality assumes suffering itself is evil, from which it follows that inflicting suffering (violence) must always be evil, and pity for suffering must be the highest virtue. Ilyin directly denies the axiom: suffering is not evil but "the price of spirituality," the very thing that closes off the easy path to lower pleasures and opens, for those with the right inner orientation, the hard path upward toward genuine spiritual life — "to god comes only that part of the soul which has not found pleasure and comfort" in mere earthly satisfaction. The chapter closes by naming what has been lost: a spiritually empty love that offers only "mutual animal pity," poured out equally on the righteous and the villain alike, which Ilyin calls humiliating to both the lover and the beloved, since real love wills the beloved's spiritual perfection, not merely the absence of his suffering.

Main Points

1. Elevating Moral Experience Above All Else Produces a Cascading Nihilism

Core Argument: When personal moral experience is placed above religion, science, art, law, and patriotism as the supreme criterion for judging all of them, each domain is devastated in turn — religious mystery dismissed as incomprehensible, scientific truth reduced to moral utility, art judged only by its moral usefulness, and law, state, and homeland rejected wholesale as institutionalized violence — leaving a person stripped of the entire "storehouse of spiritual culture."

Historical Context: Ilyin is showing the full cost of the moral egocentrism diagnosed in Chapters 9-10 — it is not a contained error about how to treat other people but a totalizing framework that consumes every other source of spiritual meaning available to a human life.

Biblical Foundation: Paul's vision of the many gifts and functions within the one Body (1 Corinthians 12:4-27) — prophecy, teaching, healing, governance, each valuable in its own right — stands opposed to Tolstoy's flattening of every domain into a single moral yardstick; Scripture itself presupposes the legitimate, non-reducible plurality of spiritual and social goods Ilyin defends.

Patristic Witness: The Church Fathers' own integration of philosophy, natural science, and the arts into a Christian synthesis (rather than their wholesale rejection) — visible from the Cappadocians' use of Greek learning onward — models the alternative to Tolstoy's nihilistic reduction: a spiritually ordered culture need not devalue every domain that does not directly serve individual moral improvement.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Religious nihilism: the moralist "examines and analyzes the dogmas and rights of the Christian Church, dismissing everything that he finds strange and incomprehensible," mistaking his own myopia for critical honesty.
  • B. Scientific and aesthetic nihilism: truth and beauty are accepted only insofar as they serve moral utility, and rejected as "idle," "vain," or "depraved" otherwise — even when this verdict comes "in spite of aesthetic bankruptcy" in the art it approves.
  • C. Juridical-state-patriotic nihilism: the "function of protection... of suppression... of the court... of punishment... of the sword" all evoke only "disgust and searing indignation," and the defense of the homeland itself is denied as ever being worth dying for.

Practical Application: A moral framework's health can be tested by whether it can hold multiple genuine goods (truth, beauty, justice, love) together without collapsing them all into a single criterion — a worldview that must devalue everything except its own central concern is, on Ilyin's argument, already a sign of distortion.

Catechumenate Note: This offers an important caution against a reductive piety that treats theology, liturgical beauty, and the Church's canonical and institutional life as mere obstacles to "simple" moral goodness — Orthodoxy's fullness includes dogma, art, and order as genuine goods in their own right, not decorations on an essentially moral core.

2. Suffering Is Not Evil — It Is the Price of Spirituality

Core Argument: Sentimental morality rests on a hidden, false axiom — that suffering itself is evil — from which everything else follows (violence is always evil, pity is the highest virtue); but in reality, suffering is what closes off the easy path to lower pleasures and opens the hard path upward toward genuine spiritual perfection, so that "to god comes only that part of the soul which has not found pleasure and comfort" in mere earthly satisfaction.

Historical Context: Ilyin identifies this as the deepest root of sentimental hedonism — not simply an emotional error but a metaphysical misjudgment about the actual relationship between suffering and spiritual growth.

Biblical Foundation: Romans 5:3-4's declaration that believers "glory in tribulations" because tribulation produces patience, experience, and hope directly refutes the axiom that suffering is simply evil — Scripture treats suffering as instrumental to spiritual maturation, not as an unqualified evil to be eliminated at any cost.

Patristic Witness: The whole ascetic tradition of voluntary hardship — fasting, vigil, poverty — as the deliberate embrace of suffering for the sake of spiritual ascent embodies Ilyin's claim precisely: "every truly spiritual movement and achievement grows out of suffering," and the saints are those who "did not get too used to human satisfaction... but suffered."

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin's central formula: "suffering is the price of spirituality and the limit of animality... this is the source of will and spirit, the beginning of purification and vision."
  • B. He is careful to specify this is not a program of self-torture: "this does not mean that a person will purposely torture himself," but rather that one should "overcome in himself the fear of suffering, cease to see evil in it," and even find the strength to allow suffering to himself or neighbor "in the cause of a higher spiritual need."
  • C. He states the crucial qualification: such suffering must be undertaken carefully, "taking care of one thing" — that it "does not damage the strength of spiritual witness and spiritual love in a person."

Practical Application: This reframes how believers should respond to necessary hardship — not by treating all suffering as an unqualified evil to be eliminated at any cost (including the cost of never resisting evil), but by discerning which sufferings serve genuine spiritual growth and accepting those, even imposing them when spiritually necessary, while guarding always against damage to love and clarity.

Catechumenate Note: This is directly relevant to how a catechumen should understand ascetic disciplines (fasting, vigil, almsgiving that costs something real) — these are not incidental hardships tolerated on the way to comfort, but the very "price" by which the soul is opened to genuine spiritual life, exactly as Ilyin describes.

3. Sentimental Pity Is Mere Animal Compassion, Not Spiritualized Love

Core Argument: Because sentimental morality denies the reality and value of the spiritual dimension, its "love" collapses into pity for suffering alone — poured out equally and indiscriminately on both the righteous and the villain — which Ilyin calls "mutual animal pity" and judges humiliating to both parties, since genuine love wills the beloved's spiritual perfection, not merely the cessation of his suffering.

Historical Context: This is Ilyin's summary verdict on the entire critique begun in Chapter 10 — sentimental love fails not only because it is weak-willed and egocentric but because, at its root, it has severed itself from the spiritual dimension altogether, reducing all human worth to the single axis of suffering versus pity.

Biblical Foundation: The distinction between mere compassion and true spiritual love appears in Christ's own dealings with sinners — He shows compassion to the woman caught in adultery (John 8:11) but does not stop there, commanding "go, and sin no more"; sentimental pity that stops at compassion without willing the beloved's actual spiritual transformation falls short of this pattern.

Patristic Witness: The Fathers' consistent teaching that love of neighbor means willing his salvation and spiritual growth, not merely alleviating his temporal suffering, is captured in the ascetic maxim that the highest mercy is to help a soul toward repentance, not merely toward comfort — precisely the distinction Ilyin insists sentimental pity collapses.

Sub-Points:

  • A. Ilyin's sharp formula: this love "is not a meeting with the divine in the joint test and vision of him... [but] mutual relaxation in mutual animal pity."
  • B. Such love "poured equally into the meek and the villain... harms both" — because it fails to distinguish or address the spiritual reality of either party.
  • C. Ilyin concludes: love "for one's neighbor is love for his spirit and his spirituality, not just pity for his suffering animality" — and love that stops short of this "is humiliating for both the beloved and the lover."

Practical Application: Genuine compassion for someone's suffering should be a starting point, not the whole of love — the deeper question is always whether one wills the other's actual spiritual growth and good, which may sometimes require actions (correction, resistance, refusal to indulge) that mere pity would never permit.

Catechumenate Note: This distinguishes Christian love clearly from a generic, secular compassion that treats the reduction of suffering as the highest achievable good — Orthodox love aims higher, at theosis and the transformation of the whole person, of which the relief of suffering is only ever a partial and sometimes even secondary concern.

Bible Verse Deep Dives

Romans 5:3-4

  • Context: Paul's teaching that believers glory in tribulations because of the chain of spiritual formation they produce — patience, experience, hope.
  • Theological Significance: Directly contradicts the axiom that suffering is simply evil, instead framing it as instrumentally valuable to spiritual maturation.
  • Use in Chapter: Provides the clearest scriptural refutation of the "hidden axiom" Ilyin identifies at the root of sentimental morality — that suffering is not, in fact, an unqualified evil.
  • Cross-References: James 1:2-4; 1 Peter 1:6-7.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

John 8:11

  • Context: Christ's response to the woman caught in adultery — mercy toward her person combined with a direct command toward repentance.
  • Theological Significance: Models a love that unites compassion with a clear, active will for the beloved's spiritual transformation, not compassion alone.
  • Use in Chapter: Illustrates the pattern Ilyin says sentimental pity fails to reach — genuine love does not stop at feeling sorry for suffering but wills and works toward the beloved's actual spiritual good.
  • Cross-References: Luke 7:47-50; John 5:14.
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — New Testament text.

Orthodox Lens

Liturgical Connection: The Church's liturgical year, structured around fasting seasons that deliberately impose real hardship before the feasts of joy, embodies Ilyin's claim that suffering, rightly ordered, is not an evil to be avoided but a discipline that opens the way to genuine spiritual ascent.

Ascetic Formation: The whole tradition of voluntary ascetic hardship — vigil, fasting, poverty, prostration — stands as a lived refutation of the axiom that suffering is simply evil; the ascetics embraced hardship precisely because, as Ilyin argues, "to god comes only that part of the soul which has not found pleasure and comfort" too easily.

Sacramental Theology: The distinction between the mercy of absolution (which restores communion) and mere sentimental tolerance of sin (which leaves the sinner unchanged) reflects Ilyin's insistence that love for a person's spirit, not just pity for his suffering, is the true measure of Christian charity.

Patristic Harmony: St. Isaac the Syrian's teaching that the "merciful heart" burns with compassion even for demons, yet this compassion never collapses into indifference to the difference between good and evil, embodies the fuller, spiritualized love Ilyin contrasts with mere undiscriminating "animal pity."

Thematic Concept Analysis

1. Moral Reductionism as a Total Cultural Devastation.
Definition: elevating personal moral experience above all other spiritual goods, judging and devaluing religion, science, art, law, and homeland by its narrow criterion.
Development through the chapter: the sequential catalogue of religious, scientific, aesthetic, and juridical-state-patriotic nihilism.
Implications: warns against any reductive worldview, religious or secular, that collapses the genuine plurality of human goods into a single overriding concern.

2. Suffering as the Necessary Price of Spiritual Ascent.
Definition: suffering closes the easy path to lower pleasures and is the necessary, though not sufficient, condition for genuine spiritual growth.
Development: the direct refutation of the "suffering is evil" axiom, and the careful qualification that suffering must not damage love and clarity.
Implications: provides the metaphysical foundation the whole book has been building toward — that force, which necessarily involves inflicting some suffering, cannot be ruled out simply because it causes hardship.

3. Animal Pity Versus Spiritualized Love.
Definition: a love that addresses only the beloved's suffering, indiscriminately, without willing his actual spiritual transformation.
Development: the closing indictment of sentimental love as "mutual animal pity" that "harms both" the righteous and the villain by treating them identically.
Implications: sharpens the definition of good as spiritualized love (Ch. 3) by showing precisely what falls short of it — mere compassion untethered from a will for the other's spiritual good.

Reflection Questions

  1. (Comprehension) What four forms of "nihilism" does Ilyin say result from elevating personal moral experience above religion, science, art, and the state?
  2. (Comprehension) What is the "hidden axiom" Ilyin identifies at the root of sentimental morality, and how does he refute it?
  3. (Theological/Analytical) Ilyin argues suffering is "the price of spirituality." How would you distinguish this claim from a dangerous glorification of suffering for its own sake, given his own qualification that suffering must never damage love and clarity?
  4. (Theological/Analytical) How does Romans 5:3-4's account of tribulation producing hope support or complicate Ilyin's broader argument that force, which necessarily causes some suffering, cannot be ruled out on that basis alone?
  5. (Personal/Devotional) Can you recall a period of real hardship in your own life that, looking back, opened a path to spiritual growth you would not otherwise have taken?
  6. (Personal/Devotional) Ilyin distinguishes "animal pity" from spiritualized love that wills the beloved's actual transformation. Have you ever offered someone comfort when what they actually needed was a harder truth or a real correction?
  7. (Liturgical/Sacramental) How does the Church's rhythm of fasting before feasting embody Ilyin's claim that suffering, properly ordered, is not evil but a discipline opening the way to joy?
