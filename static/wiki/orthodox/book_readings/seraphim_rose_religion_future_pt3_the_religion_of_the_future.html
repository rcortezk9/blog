"Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world."
— 1 John 4:1, the verse this entire book has been building toward applying to the Church's own parishes

Before you read: Parts 1 and 2 tested Hinduism, meditation movements, and UFO phenomena — all things happening, in Rose's telling, mostly outside or alongside the Church. This final movement turns the same diagnostic tools on something already inside Orthodox parishes in Rose's own lifetime: the charismatic revival. This is the chapter that cost the book real friction within American Orthodoxy, and it's where the book's title finally gets explained rather than implied. Read slowly — this is the argument's destination, not a detour.

Overview

Chapters 7 and 8, together with the Epilogue, are where Fr. Seraphim Rose states outright what Parts 1 and 2 have been assembling evidence for. Chapter 7 subjects the charismatic revival — by 1979 already present in Greek and Syrian Orthodox parishes in America — to the same tests applied to Hinduism and meditation: origin, mechanism, and fruit. Chapter 8 names the conclusion plainly: the charismatic revival, Christian meditation, and the broader "new religious consciousness" surveyed across the whole book are not disconnected curiosities but a single, converging phenomenon — the religion of the future, preparing mankind, nominal Christians very much included, to receive Antichrist as though he were Christ returned. The Epilogue, added for a later edition after the November 1978 Jonestown massacre, is not a postscript. It is Rose's proof that the pattern he's describing is not theoretical.

Chapters in This Part

  • Ch. 7, The Charismatic Revival — Pentecostal origins, speaking in tongues, and the case that the movement is mediumistic rather than genuinely charismatic (in the sense of the Holy Spirit's gifts)
  • Ch. 8, The Spirit of the Last Times — the book's thesis stated directly: this is the religion of the future, and Orthodoxy's answer is not competition but sober watchfulness
  • Epilogue, Jonestown in the 1980s — the November 1978 mass suicide read as the pattern already fully enacted

Main Points

1. Pentecostalism's Origin and a Telling Statistical Clue

Core Argument: Rose traces the movement to a precise date — December 31, 1900, Topeka, Kansas — when Charles Parham's Bible students, having deduced from Acts that tongues must accompany the Spirit's reception, prayed until one girl spoke in an unknown language after the laying on of hands. From there it spread through Los Angeles revivalism to become, by the 1970s, a movement claiming tens of millions worldwide and official approval even from Rome. Rose's sharpest observation is diagnostic rather than historical: David du Plessis, a leading Pentecostal figure, notes the revival is strongest precisely in "the so-called liberal societies," and "found much less" in evangelical or fundamentalist Protestant circles — exactly reversed from what you'd expect if the phenomenon were a recovery of apostolic zeal rather than a product of theological looseness meeting a hunger for experience.

Biblical Foundation: Rose repeatedly returns to 1 Corinthians 12–14, noting Paul mentions tongues only to restrain a source of disorder in Corinth, never to encourage it — the opposite of how contemporary Pentecostal apologetics use the passage.

Practical Application / Catechumenate Note: The "strongest where doctrine is loosest" pattern is worth generalizing beyond Pentecostalism specifically: it's a genuinely useful diagnostic question to bring to any spiritually intense movement you encounter — not "does this produce powerful experiences" but "where, specifically, does it take root, and does that distribution make sense if the explanation is 'the Holy Spirit is moving,' or does it make more sense under some other explanation?"

Patristic Witness: St. Augustine, in his homilies on John, is quoted directly stating that tongues in the apostolic era "were a sign adapted to the time... and it passed away," and pointedly asking whether anyone, on witnessing confirmation without tongues accompanying it, would dare conclude the person "had not received the Holy Spirit." Rose uses Augustine to establish that the Church's own historic mind already answered the "is tongues normative" question sixteen centuries before Topeka.

2. The Mediumism Argument: Point-for-Point Structural Parallels

Core Argument: This is the chapter's technical center and its most uncomfortable move. Rose lays a standard spiritistic-séance manual (with its named prerequisites — passivity, group solidarity of belief, a "magnetic circle" formed by joined hands, an induced atmosphere via music and suggestion) directly alongside charismatic prayer-meeting practice, prerequisite by prerequisite, and finds them matching almost exactly: the séance's passivity is the charismatic "letting go"; the séance's solidarity in faith (a skeptic's presence can abort a séance) matches the charismatic testimony that one doubter present can prevent the baptism of the Holy Spirit from "taking"; the magnetic circle is the laying on of hands; the induced atmosphere is the buildup of suggestive hymns and hand-clapping. He then cites a German Lutheran pastor's careful study (Dr. Kurt Koch) of hundreds of tongues cases, concluding that the overwhelming majority — Koch's own figure is roughly 95% — are mediumistic in character rather than either genuine gift or outright demonic possession.

Biblical Foundation: Deuteronomy 18:10–12 and Leviticus 20:6 (prohibitions on mediums and "familiar spirits") frame the whole comparison — Rose's claim is not that charismatics are consciously practicing necromancy, but that the mechanism they've adopted is functionally identical to it regardless of the sincere Christian intention layered on top.

Practical Application / Catechumenate Note: This is the hardest part of the book to sit with honestly if you have any charismatic or Pentecostal background or relationships, because Rose is not attacking insincerity — he explicitly addresses readers who've had real, moving experiences in the movement, telling them their turn toward repentance and love of God was genuine even if its packaging was not: "all this is indeed good and would not be lost by abandoning the charismatic movement." That's a model worth keeping for how you extend charity to people whose spiritual experience you have real theological reservations about — you don't have to deny the fruit was in any sense real to still name the root as mistaken.

Patristic Witness: St. John Cassian's Conferences on discerning spirits is invoked for the observation that demons sometimes work real wonders "to lift into pride the man who believes himself to possess the miraculous gift," a warning Rose reads as directly applicable to the charismatic movement's emphasis on spiritual "boldness" and confidence as proof of the Spirit's presence.

3. Prelest, Fully Applied: Bishop Ignatius Brianchaninov's Two Forms

Core Argument: Rose systematically applies the two-form taxonomy of spiritual deception he introduced via prelest back in Chapter 2 — now drawn out fully from 19th-century Bishop Ignatius (Brianchaninov)'s collected works. The first, more dramatic form (mnenie) grants visions to those who seek high spiritual states without prior purification — illustrated by St. Nikitas of Novgorod, deceived for years by a "praying angel" until his aversion to the New Testament exposed the fraud. The second, more common and more dangerous form (called "fancy," a warm, comfortable counterfeit of grace) requires no dramatic vision at all — only "exalted religious feelings" in a heart not yet purified by real repentance. Rose walks through charismatic testimony under this second category point by point: uncontrollable laughter ("the laughter of the Holy Spirit" — which Bishop Ignatius, quoting the desert tradition, calls flatly incompatible with the fear of God), tears without evident cause, physical warmth and trembling, sudden "boldness," and above all direct first-person "words from the Lord" delivered through interpretation of tongues — vague, stereotyped, always consoling, never doctrinally or prophetically specific in the way Old Testament prophecy actually was.

Biblical Foundation: Jeremiah 29:8–9 — "let not your prophets... deceive you, for they prophesy falsely" — is applied directly to the charismatic "words from the Lord." Matthew 5:4 ("blessed are they that mourn") is set against the uncontrolled laughter as evidence the phenomenon inverts, rather than fulfills, the Beatitudes' actual shape of blessedness.

Practical Application / Catechumenate Note: The single most transferable tool in this whole book, for you personally, is Bishop Ignatius's principle that the awareness that you yourself could be deceived is itself the primary safeguard against deception — "it is the greatest deception to acknowledge oneself to be free of deception." That's not a counsel of anxious self-doubt about every prayerful feeling you have. It's a habit of testing consolation against the actual fruit it produces over time (does it deepen repentance, scripture-reading, and humility, or does it, as Rose notes happened to several of his sources, quietly erode the desire to pray and read the Bible at all?) rather than trusting intensity of feeling as self-authenticating.

Patristic Witness: St. John Climacus (The Ladder) is quoted directly: "Do not trust your fountains of tears before your soul has been perfectly purified," and, more starkly, "tears without thought are proper only to an irrational nature." St. Barsanuphius and John (6th c.) are cited on laughter specifically: "in the fear of God there is no laughter... laughter offends the Holy Spirit... destroys what has been built up."

4. "A Pentecost Without Christ": The Movement's Own Unmasking

Core Argument: Rose's most striking piece of evidence is not his own argument but a charismatic apologist's own unguarded account: a man who received "the baptism of the Holy Spirit" experience without repentance, without any resulting desire to be freed from sin, and without Christ functioning as anything necessary to the experience at all — described candidly by the same movement's own writer as "a Pentecost without repentance, a Pentecost without Christ." Rose's conclusion follows directly: if the defining experience of the movement can occur, by its own witnesses' admission, with no necessary connection to Christ at all, then the experience is not, in fact, a Christian phenomenon that happens to use Christian vocabulary — it's a free-floating psychic/spiritual technique for which Christian content is optional packaging. This is, structurally, the exact same finding as Chapter 4's diagnosis of Christian yoga and Christian Zen: technique detached from the metaphysics that would actually make it Christian.

Practical Application / Catechumenate Note: This is worth holding onto as a portable test, well beyond the charismatic movement specifically: when you encounter any practice or experience marketed as distinctively Christian, it's fair to ask — could this exact experience, by its own proponents' account, occur just as easily without any real reference to Christ, repentance, or the sacramental life of the Church? If yes, the Christian label is doing less work than it appears to.

5. The Thesis, Stated Directly: This Is the Religion of the Future

Core Argument: Chapter 8 is where the book's title stops being a promise and becomes an argument. Rose's claim, stated without hedging: the charismatic revival, Christian meditation, and the broader new religious consciousness surveyed across the whole book are "forerunners of the religion of the future, the religion of the last humanity, the religion of antichrist," and their "chief spiritual function is to make available to Christians the demonic initiation hitherto restricted to the pagan world." Crucially, Rose does not claim most participants are consciously worshipping Satan, or that most meditation and tongues experiences are full demonic possession — he explicitly allows for large amounts of ordinary psychological self-deception mixed in with the genuinely preternatural cases. His point is about direction and function, not universal individual culpability: these movements, taken together, are training an entire generation — inside and outside the Church — in passivity, openness, and hunger for signs, which is precisely the disposition 2 Thessalonians 2 describes as necessary soil for Antichrist's "lying wonders."

Biblical Foundation: 2 Thessalonians 2:3–4, 9–12 is the chapter's central text, quoted at length — apostasy first, then "the man of sin... whose coming is after the working of Satan, with all power and signs and lying wonders." Luke 12:32 ("fear not, little flock") and Romans 11:4 ("seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal") are Rose's counter-image: not a triumphalist Orthodoxy, but a small, faithful remnant.

Practical Application / Catechumenate Note: This is the point in the book where its actual pastoral offer becomes visible, and it's worth naming clearly for yourself as a catechumen: Rose is not telling you Orthodoxy is the better spiritual technique, the more powerful competing experience, the tradition with the real miracles as opposed to everyone else's fake ones. His entire argument up to here has been that the appetite for that kind of comparison — which tradition delivers the most intense, most convincing spiritual experience — is itself the trap. His actual counsel is almost anti-climactic by design: hold the grace already given you at chrismation; don't let it "become a matter of habit"; don't expect it to be "logical or comprehensible" by the standards of what feels spiritually impressive. That's a genuinely different posture than "come to Orthodoxy because it works better," and it's worth letting that distinction reshape how you think about your own reception into the Church — not as acquiring access to superior spiritual experiences, but as being placed inside a sober, watchful inheritance that doesn't need to compete on those terms at all.

Patristic Witness: St. Seraphim of Sarov is invoked directly against the charismatics' own selective quotation of him — his actual teaching in On the Acquisition of the Holy Spirit ties the grace given at baptism explicitly to its sealing "by the sacrament of chrismation... appointed by the holy Church," a sacramental and ecclesial grounding the charismatic movement's citations of him conveniently omit.

6. Epilogue: Jonestown as the Pattern Already Enacted

Core Argument: Added after the November 1978 mass suicide of over 900 people at Jim Jones's Jonestown commune, the Epilogue is Rose's claim that this was not an isolated act of madness but a concrete, historical instance of exactly the mechanism the whole book has described. Jones, Rose notes, had a documented background in 1950s spiritualism and openly described himself as "an oracle or medium for discarnate entities from another galaxy" — the same mediumistic structure diagnosed in the charismatic movement, now fully unmasked and taken to its logical terminus. Rose draws the parallel deliberately to Dostoevsky's The Possessed (specifically the character Kirillov, who believes suicide is the ultimate proof he has become God) and to the mass political violence of the Cambodian genocide occurring in the same period — reading both as instances of a single modern pattern: religious fervor and utopian political logic fusing into something willing to enact "a purified humanity" through mass death, prefiguring what Rose expects the specifically religious-political figure of Antichrist to combine.

Practical Application / Catechumenate Note: The Epilogue's real function for you as a reader isn't to make a claim about Jonestown specifically (a 1978 event, now historically distant) — it's to close the book by insisting the argument was never abstract. Rose wants you to leave Orthodoxy and the Religion of the Future not with a tidy theological taxonomy but with genuine sobriety about what unexamined openness to "spiritual power" can produce when it runs all the way to its end. That's the right note to actually finish this study on: not fear, but the "fear and trembling" of Philippians 2:12 — a serious, watchful, unspectacular faithfulness, which is the entire book's real alternative to every "religion of the future" it surveys.

Combating Prelest — A Practical Rule

The book doesn't leave the diagnosis without a method. Gathered across Chapters 2–8, here is what Rose actually tells the reader to do — not a mood, a rule:

  1. Test everything by fruit, not by intensity of experience. The operative verse throughout is 1 John 4:1 — "try the spirits whether they are of God." The concrete tool for how comes from St. Gregory of Sinai (Ch. 2): grace produces meekness, humility, cutting off of the passions; the counterfeit produces arrogance, conceit, intimidation — however luminous the experience felt in the moment. Lettuce that looks like mustard, vinegar that looks like wine — distinguishable only by taste, never by appearance.
  2. Assume you yourself could be deceived — that assumption is the actual defense. Bishop Ignatius Brianchaninov's line, quoted repeatedly across Ch. 7–8: "It is the greatest deception to acknowledge oneself to be free of deception." Not anxious self-doubt about every prayer — a standing posture that refuses to treat your own certainty as proof.
  3. Submit experience to a living guide rather than judging it alone. Both hagiographical cases Rose cites (St. Nikitas of Novgorod in Ch. 2, St. Isaac of the Kiev Caves) end the same way: the deception is broken only by someone outside the deceived person. Private spiritual experience judged only by the one having it is exactly the mechanism prelest exploits — hence Rose's repeated insistence on confession and a spiritual father as structural safeguards, not mere piety.
  4. Read the actual Holy Fathers whole, not excerpts assembled to fit a conclusion. Rose's specific complaint against the charismatics (Ch. 7) is that they quote St. Seraphim of Sarov and St. Symeon the New Theologian with the inconvenient parts cut — St. Seraphim's own words tie the gift of the Spirit explicitly to chrismation "as appointed by the holy Church," which the charismatic excerpts omit. The remedy: the Philokalia, the Lausiac History, St. John Climacus's Ladder, St. John Cassian's Conferences, the lives of the desert Fathers, Unseen Warfare, St. John of Kronstadt's My Life in Christ — read entire, not mined for supporting lines.
  5. Hold the grace already given — don't chase a more impressive one. The book's actual closing counsel (Ch. 8), deliberately anticlimactic: "Hold fast to the grace which you have. Never let it become a matter of habit. Never measure it by merely human standards or expect it to be logical or comprehensible... or think to obtain the grace of the Holy Spirit in some other way than that which the one Church of Christ has handed down to us." The answer to spiritual hunger isn't a better competing intensity — it's fidelity to what's already been given at baptism and chrismation.
  6. Expect to be a minority, not a majority — and treat that as normal, not alarming. Romans 11:4 ("seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal") and Luke 12:32 ("fear not, little flock") are Rose's closing self-image: watchfulness without triumphalism.

Further scriptural anchors for the "how to stand firm" question specifically: 2 Thessalonians 2:15 ("hold to the traditions which you have been taught"), Galatians 1:8-9 (anathema on any other gospel, even from "an angel from heaven"), Matthew 24:24 (even the elect can be deceived — so don't presume immunity), Hebrews 13:17 (submission to those who watch over your soul).

Reflection Questions

  1. Rose extends real charity to charismatics who found genuine repentance through the movement, even while rejecting the movement's theology. Where in your own life do you need to practice that same distinction — separating a real fruit from a mistaken root — toward someone else's faith experience?
  2. The mediumism parallel (passivity, solidarity, magnetic circle, induced atmosphere) is uncomfortably precise. Does it hold up as genuine structural analysis, or does it prove too much — could it be turned, unfairly, against forms of prayer Rose himself would defend?
  3. "It is the greatest deception to acknowledge oneself to be free of deception." How do you actually practice that kind of ongoing self-suspicion without it curdling into scrupulosity or a paralyzing distrust of every consolation in prayer?
  4. Rose's positive counsel in Chapter 8 is strikingly unspectacular — hold the grace you have, don't expect it to be impressive. Does that land, for you, as a relief or as an anticlimax after everything the book has surveyed?
  5. Now that you've read the whole book as a single argument: what is "the religion of the future," in one sentence, in your own words — and what do you think Rose would say is the one thing that actually distinguishes Orthodoxy's answer from just being a better-run version of the same impulse?
