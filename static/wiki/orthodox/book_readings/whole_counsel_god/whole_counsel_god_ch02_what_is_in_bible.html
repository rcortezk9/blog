"The Church does not receive her faith from the Scriptures; she receives the Scriptures within her faith."
— paraphrased from the Orthodox catechetical tradition, echoing St. Irenaeus

Before you read: This is a dense, detail-heavy chapter, and the temptation will be to read it for facts rather than for its argument. Resist that. Underneath the history of Torah, Prophets, Writings, Josephus, and the Council of Nicea myth, De Young is making one sustained claim: canon is received, not decided. Let the sheer messiness of the historical record — the Dead Sea Scrolls, the varying Old Testament lists across Orthodox local churches, the absence of any Nicene vote — work on you rather than resolving it into a tidy slogan too quickly. A canon that came to be this organically, this communally, without a central committee, is a different kind of authority than a canon handed down by decree — dwell in that difference.

Chapter Overview

Chapter 2 dismantles two popular myths about how the biblical canon came to be — that the Old Testament existed as a single agreed-upon Hebrew unit before Christianity, and that the New Testament's twenty-seven books were voted into existence at the Council of Nicea in AD 325 — and replaces them with a patient, evidence-driven account of organic reception. De Young walks through the tripartite structure of the Hebrew Scriptures (Torah, Prophets, Writings), the messiness of Second Temple Jewish textual plurality (illustrated by Qumran), the specific and limited disputes that separate Orthodox, Catholic, and Protestant Old Testaments, and then the New Testament's remarkably early and widespread agreement on nineteen of twenty-seven books, with the remaining eight (the "disputed" books, a term De Young calls a modern mistranslation of Eusebius) settling into place by the sixth century. The chapter's theological payoff, delivered in its final paragraph, is that canon formation itself is an act of the Holy Spirit operating through the shared life of the Church — Holy Tradition — rather than the verdict of any single council, scholar, or ancient witness like Josephus.

Main Points

Point 1: The Old Testament Canon Was Never a Single Pre-Christian Unit

Core Argument: The idea that "the Hebrew Bible" existed as an agreed, closed unit before Christ, which Christians then simply inherited and added to, is factually false — first-century Judaism had no single agreed canon, no authoritative master list, and multiple competing communities with different collections.

Historical Context: Before the codex, Old Testament texts circulated as individual scrolls. A synagogue might own only a Torah scroll; a well-funded one might add Psalms and Isaiah. Only major centers like Jerusalem or Alexandria held anything approaching the "full" range of texts. The temple's own scroll collection in Jerusalem — sometimes cited by modern scholars as evidence of a first-century canon — is a weak witness: it doesn't match any later canon precisely, was largely inaccessible to diaspora Jews, and a significant number of Jewish groups (rejecting Herod's temple, its non-Zadokite priesthood, or its perceived illegitimacy under Roman occupation) didn't recognize its authority at all.

Biblical Foundation: The layered composition of Isaiah (collected by disciples per Is. 8:16–20), the two divergent Hebrew forms of Jeremiah, and the five originally independent "books" within the Psalter (with differing Hebrew/Greek verse numbering) are cited as internal evidence against a monolithic, pre-existing canon.

Patristic Witness: Not directly cited here, though the chapter's method — reading Scripture's own composition history as evidence — mirrors patristic comfort with textual complexity that later Western polemics would flatten into simpler claims.

Sub-Points:

  • A. The Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran preserve one such alternative community canon — containing the Torah and Prophets, some Writings shared with later canons, others (like 1 Enoch, found in greater quantity than Genesis or Exodus) never received elsewhere.
  • B. Josephus (a Pharisee) presents his own party's canon of twenty-two books in Against Apion as if universally held by all Jews — a claim De Young shows to be self-serving hyperbole, contradicted by the diversity of Second Temple Jewish textual practice.
  • C. Esther is a notable case: it existed in at least two significantly different first-century versions, one becoming part of the Hebrew Bible, the other part of the Christian Old Testament; it is also entirely absent from Qumran, along with any trace of Purim in the community's festal calendar.

Practical Application: Knowing that the "Old Testament canon" was never a settled monolith before Christ frees the Orthodox Christian from having to defend an artificial uniformity — and instead invites appreciation for how the Church, guided by the Spirit, received a particular, textured inheritance rather than rubber-stamping someone else's finished list.

Catechumenate Note: If you were taught that the "Old Testament" is simply "the Jewish Bible" that Christians later supplemented, this section corrects that picture at the root. The Orthodox Old Testament is not a borrowed artifact; it is the Church's own reception, through the apostles, of a living and variegated Second Temple textual inheritance — read now, as always, through Christ.

Point 2: Torah, Prophets, and Writings — The Three-Part Shape of the Hebrew Scriptures

Core Argument: The Old Testament's three traditional divisions — Torah (Law), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings) — developed and stabilized at different rates and for different reasons, with the Writings remaining the most fluid and least uniformly received division well into the Christian era.

Historical Context: The Torah's five books, though of Mosaic origin, show clear later editorial updating (e.g., the anachronistic city name "Dan" in Gen. 14:14). The Prophets divide into "Former" (Joshua–2 Kings, essentially theologically interpreted history written from Deuteronomy's perspective — the so-called "Deuteronomistic History") and "Latter" (the major and minor prophets proper). The Writings are a catchall — poetry, wisdom literature, apocalyptic, historical narrative, love song — that resist easy classification and were the last division to stabilize, differently, in different communities.

Biblical Foundation: Deuteronomy 31:11–12 commands the Torah be read publicly to the whole assembly — "men, women, and children... and the sojourner within your towns" — establishing the pattern of Scripture's public, liturgical reading in worship that "has endured throughout Christian history." The contrast between 1 Kings/3 Kingdoms' eight dismissive verses on King Omri (historically one of Israel's most consequential kings) versus its lavish treatment of more theologically significant but politically minor kings illustrates the Deuteronomistic History's theological, not merely factual, historiography.

Patristic Witness: The Church's continued liturgical practice of publicly reading the Prophets and Writings (e.g., at Vespers before feasts) is presented as direct continuation of the Deuteronomy 31 pattern, connecting Old Testament liturgical practice to Orthodox worship today.

Sub-Points:

  • A. The "documentary hypothesis" (J, E, D, P sources) is treated critically: originally constructed to defend single Mosaic authorship, its later development by critical scholars removed authorship from Moses' era entirely, though De Young notes the theory's classic form has become "considerably more restrained" as its complexity multiplied implausibly.
  • B. The Book of Psalms exemplifies internal compositional plurality — five smaller "books" within it, attributed across a thousand-year span, with differing Hebrew/Greek verse numbering reflecting the union or division of certain psalm texts.
  • C. Wisdom literature (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Wisdom of Solomon, Ben Sirach, Job) clusters loosely around the figure of Solomon without uniform authorship claims — a pattern the Fathers understood as thematic and traditional attribution rather than literal authorship.

Practical Application: Understanding the different textual histories of Torah, Prophets, and Writings equips the Orthodox reader to hold different parts of the Old Testament with different expectations — the same close scrutiny of "which version, which community" that a scholar brings to the Writings is simply not needed for the Torah and Prophets, which were far more universally received.

Catechumenate Note: This granularity matters practically: when you notice that your Orthodox Study Bible includes books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees) that a Protestant Bible you may have grown up with does not, this chapter explains precisely why — not because the Orthodox Church added books, but because these texts were received, as part of the Writings, by the apostolic Church from the wider Greek-speaking Jewish textual tradition it inherited.

Point 3: The Orthodox Old Testament — Received Diversity, Not Manufactured Uniformity

Core Argument: All Orthodox local churches share the same twenty-two (or thirty-nine, by modern division) universally accepted books plus seven additional texts (Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Ben Sirach, Baruch, Epistle of Jeremiah), but beyond this core, minor variation persists between local churches — a variation the East has never sought to forcibly resolve, unlike the West.

Historical Context: Different pre-Christian Jewish communities (Palestinian, Alexandrian Egyptian, Ethiopian) received different additional book-sets alongside the shared core, and the Christian communities that grew out of each simply inherited that variation. Egypt, for instance, received an extensive additional list, though St. Athanasius (in his Paschal Letter 39) placed several of these — Tobit, Judith, Esther, Wisdom, Ben Sirach, along with the Didache and Shepherd of Hermas — into a "second rank," neither publicly read in church nor entirely banned, a category distinct from St. Cyril of Jerusalem's own differing classification of the same territory. The most expansively canoned churches, Ethiopian and Coptic, left communion with Chalcedonian Orthodoxy after AD 451, meaning the variation among communing Orthodox churches today is comparatively narrow.

Biblical Foundation: No single verse anchors this section; the argument is historical-ecclesiological, tracing how "canonicity" functioned locally and pastorally (books "read in church," "read in the home," "not to be read") rather than through a universal, binding master list.

Patristic Witness: St. Athanasius's Paschal Letter 39 and St. Cyril of Jerusalem's Catechetical Lectures 4.33–36 are directly cited as evidence of this graded, locally variable reception — with St. Jerome noted as translating additional books into Latin for his Vulgate despite personally not considering them canonical for the churches.

Sub-Points:

  • A. The functional distinction between "canonicity" and "deutero-canonicity" is, per De Young, "negligible" for the Old Testament — many fully canonical Orthodox books are simply never read liturgically, which does not diminish their canonical status.
  • B. The Slavonic tradition additionally includes 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151, and (via the old Georgian text) 4 Ezra and 4 Maccabees.
  • C. Because there has been no active effort by one local Orthodox church to compel another to add or reject texts, "the precise list of books in a Christian's Old Testament is a function of his or her community and the traditional biblical and liturgical language of that community" — canon, in Orthodoxy, is ecclesially located rather than universally codified down to the last book.

Practical Application: This should reshape how an Orthodox Christian discusses canon with Protestant or Catholic interlocutors — the goal is not to defend a single fixed international Orthodox list against theirs, but to explain a genuinely different model of how canonical authority works: locally received, historically continuous, pastorally graded.

Catechumenate Note: You do not need to memorize a single universal "Orthodox canon" as though it were an item on a checklist equivalent to the Protestant sixty-six or Catholic seventy-three. Learn instead the logic of reception this chapter describes — because that logic, not a fixed number, is what you are being catechized into.

Point 4: The New Testament Canon — Organic Agreement, Not a Nicene Vote

Core Argument: The popular narrative that the twenty-seven-book New Testament canon was decided by episcopal vote at Nicea in AD 325 (with a subsequent Constantinian book-burning) is, in De Young's words, "grounded entirely in fiction" — the canon was never discussed at Nicea, and nineteen of the twenty-seven books were already in near-universal use by local churches decades, even a century, earlier, entirely apart from any central authority.

Historical Context: In the earliest centuries, no central church authority existed beyond local bishops; regional councils carried no binding force over uninvolved bishops. Yet by roughly AD 150, the four Gospels were already being bound together in codices and used exclusively in major churches like Rome and Antioch — not because those cities held special authority, but because their communities retained living memory verifying which texts the apostles themselves had actually delivered. By AD 100, Paul's epistles were already circulating as a collected unit (attested by an early manuscript, Chester Beatty Papyrus 46). The eight "disputed" books — the General Epistles and Revelation — were not seriously contested for centuries so much as unevenly known: communities that had only ever received one epistle of St. John, for instance, had simply never encountered 2 and 3 John, not rejected them.

Biblical Foundation: 2 Peter 3:16, cited earlier in the chapter as evidence Peter already classed Paul's letters as "Scripture," reappears here as proof that collective scriptural status for New Testament texts predates any formal canonical process by decades.

Patristic Witness: St. Irenaeus of Lyons (Against Heresies 3.1.1; 5.30.3) is the chapter's key second-century witness — dating St. Mark's Gospel to roughly AD 69 (written after the deaths of Sts. Peter and Paul) and giving the most precise date of any NT text, placing St. John's reception of the Revelation near the end of Domitian's reign, c. AD 95. Irenaeus is also cited for recognizing that Valentinian Gnostic communities were not authentically Christian churches precisely because they used a different set of texts and held a fundamentally different faith — canon functioning here as a marker of ecclesial identity, not an abstract literary judgment. St. Clement of Alexandria's commentary on the seven General Epistles as a unit witnesses their collected circulation by the end of the second century.

Sub-Points:

  • A. The Revelation of St. John is the genuine outlier: actively rejected by many communities (associated with Montanist chiliasm) until St. Jerome's advocacy secured it in the West (5th c.) and St. Andrew of Caesarea's commentary secured it in the East (6th c.) — De Young notes it is still not read liturgically in most Orthodox churches even today.
  • B. The term "disputed books" (from Eusebius's homolegoumena/antilegomena distinction) is, per De Young, a mistranslation that overstates real controversy — Eusebius's Greek distinguishes books "about which everyone says the same thing" from those "about which people say different things," not books formally rejected and later accepted by vote.
  • C. Fathers do not speak of canonicity in terms of which books "wield some authority or show some sign of divine inspiration" but rather simply testify to which books they have received — canonicity described descriptively, not adjudicated.

Practical Application: Knowing this history equips the Orthodox Christian to respond calmly and accurately to popular skeptical narratives (Dan Brown novels, documentary claims about "banned" or "lost" gospels) — the actual historical record shows remarkably early, organic, widespread agreement, not centralized suppression.

Catechumenate Note: The New Testament you are being catechized to receive as authoritative was not handed to you by an ancient committee's majority vote; it was recognized, community by community, because those communities retained living memory of what the apostles themselves had delivered to them. You are being received into the same chain of living memory and testimony.

Bible Verse Deep Dives

Deuteronomy 31:11–12 — "You shall read this law before all Israel in their hearing... that they may hear and learn to fear the LORD your God"

  • Context: Moses' command, near the end of Deuteronomy, that the Torah be read publicly every seventh year at the Feast of Booths to the entire assembly — men, women, children, and resident aliens.
  • Theological Significance: Establishes Scripture's fundamentally liturgical, communal, oral mode of transmission and reception from its earliest institution — not private reading, but corporate hearing entrusted to the Levites and elders (paralleled, De Young notes, to New Testament deacons and presbyters).
  • Use in Chapter: Cited as the origin point of the pattern of Scripture's role in corporate worship that "has endured throughout Christian history."
  • Cross-References: Neh. 8:1–8 (Ezra's public reading of the Torah); Lk. 4:16–20 (Christ reading Isaiah in the synagogue); 1 Tim. 4:13 ("devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture").
  • LXX Note: The Greek term rendered "elders" corresponds to presbyteroi, the same word later used for Christian presbyters/priests — a linguistic continuity De Young notes explicitly, reinforcing the direct institutional line from Torah-reading Levites/elders to Gospel-reading Orthodox clergy.

2 Peter 3:16 — "As he does in all his letters... which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other Scriptures"

  • Context: Peter's closing remarks, defending Paul's letters against those who misuse difficult passages within them.
  • Theological Significance: This is the New Testament's own act of canonization-in-progress — an apostle explicitly classing a fellow apostle's letters as "Scripture" (γραφαί) within living memory of their composition.
  • Use in Chapter: Cited twice — once in Chapter 1 regarding inspiration, and again here as evidence that scriptural status for at least some Pauline letters was recognized remarkably early, within the apostolic generation itself.
  • Cross-References: 1 Tim. 5:18 (Paul quoting Luke's Gospel alongside Deuteronomy as equally authoritative "Scripture"); Col. 4:16 (letters circulating between churches).
  • LXX Note: Not applicable — this is a NT Greek text with no LXX/MT comparison.

Isaiah 8:16–20 — "Bind up the testimony; seal the teaching among my disciples"

  • Context: Isaiah, amid prophecies of judgment against Judah and Israel, instructs that his prophetic testimony be preserved and sealed among his disciples for a future generation.
  • Theological Significance: This is Scripture's own internal witness to a discipleship-based compositional process — Isaiah's prophecies were not simply written once by the prophet but collected and preserved by a community of followers, a pattern that undermines any purely individualistic model of prophetic authorship.
  • Use in Chapter: Cited as evidence that the Book of Isaiah, as we have it, is "more than one collection" gathered by his disciples after his death.
  • Cross-References: Jer. 36 (Jeremiah dictating to his scribe Baruch); Rev. 1:19 ("write what you have seen").
  • LXX Note: The LXX of Isaiah 8:16 renders "seal the teaching" (Hebrew ḥătōm tôrāh) in a way broadly consonant with the Hebrew; no major theological divergence, though the LXX's rendering of "disciples" (mathētais) uses the same word later applied to Christ's own disciples, a resonance the Fathers noted typologically.

Orthodox Lens

Liturgical Connection

The Old Testament readings at Great Vespers before major feasts (the paroimiai) directly enact this chapter's picture of canonicity as liturgical function rather than abstract list-making — a text's authority is demonstrated by its being read, chanted, and proclaimed in the Church's worship across centuries, precisely the criterion De Young identifies (Deut. 31) as the Torah's own founding pattern. The nine biblical odes sung at Matins (including the Magnificat as the ninth) — mentioned in the chapter as sometimes mistakenly assumed to be "Old Testament" material simply because they're bound into Old Testament codices — are a living example of exactly the liturgical/canonical entanglement this chapter untangles.

Ascetic Formation

The chapter's implicit lesson — that canon was received through patient, multigenerational, often locally varying fidelity rather than a single decisive act — models a form of ascetic patience relevant to spiritual life generally. Just as no single generation "finished" the canon, no single moment of ascetic effort "finishes" theosis; both are worked out across time, in communion with others, under the same Spirit.

Sacramental Theology

Understanding that different Orthodox local churches received slightly different Old Testament book-lists, without breaking communion over it, offers a model for how Orthodox unity works sacramentally — communion is grounded in shared faith and shared Eucharistic life, not textual uniformity down to the last apocryphal psalm. This softens any anxious, checklist approach to "which Bible is the real Orthodox Bible."

Patristic Harmony

St. Athanasius's Paschal Letter 39 and St. Cyril of Jerusalem's Catechetical Lectures — cited directly in the chapter — exemplify exactly the graded, pastoral, non-anxious approach to canon De Young is arguing for: books can be read profitably in the home without being read publicly in church, and this distinction carries real pastoral weight without requiring binary in/out judgments. St. Irenaeus's linkage of correct canon to ecclesial identity (Against Heresies 3.1.1) — a community's texts revealing whether it is authentically Christian or something else — grounds the New Testament section's entire argument.

Thematic Concept Analysis

1. Canon as Reception, Not Decision (κανών / kanōn)

In Orthodox context: A canon (κανών, "measuring rod" or "rule") is a set of texts that already exercises authority within a worshiping community — the community's use precedes and grounds the label "canonical," rather than an external authority declaring texts canonical and thereby creating their authority.

Development through the chapter: Both the Old and New Testament sections build to this same conclusion from different historical material — Old Testament canon as locally varied received inheritance, New Testament canon as organically converging apostolic testimony.

Soteriological implications: If canonicity is fundamentally about a community's lived, worshiping reception rather than external certification, then the individual believer's relationship to Scripture is likewise one of reception and participation, not verification from outside the Church's life.

2. Holy Tradition as the Medium of Canon Formation (Παράδοσις)

In Orthodox context: Holy Tradition names the Spirit's continuous life within the Church across generations — the same reality that produced, preserved, and eventually stabilized the biblical canon.

Development through the chapter: The chapter's closing paragraph makes this explicit: "the only entity that chose the books that would comprise our New Testament is the Holy Spirit... developed in the life of the Holy Spirit in the Church, the shared life of the Christian people, which the Orthodox Church calls Holy Tradition."

Soteriological/eschatological implications: Canon formation is thus not a closed, past-tense event disconnected from the Church's ongoing life, but a paradigm case of how the Spirit continues to guide the Church into all truth (John 16:13) across every generation.

3. The Myth of Centralized Control (Nicea, book-burning, "banned books")

In Orthodox context: Popular anti-Christian polemic (Dan Brown-style narratives) imagines an authoritarian, top-down suppression of alternative texts; the actual historical record shows the opposite — radical decentralization with remarkable, unforced convergence.

Development through the chapter: De Young methodically dismantles each piece of the popular myth: no vote at Nicea, no criteria-committee, no Constantinian book-burning, and Gnostic texts like the Gospel of Thomas were simply too late to have ever been serious candidates for a first-century apostolic canon.

Practical/apologetic implications: This equips catechumens and Orthodox Christians generally to respond to a common category of skeptical challenge with historical precision rather than defensive vagueness.

4. Graded Canonicity (books "read in church," "read in the home," "not to be read")

In Orthodox context: A three-tiered pastoral category, attested by St. Athanasius and St. Cyril of Jerusalem, that allowed real canonical variation without requiring binary rejection or forced uniformity.

Development through the chapter: Introduced specifically to explain the additional books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, etc.) beyond the twenty-two/thirty-nine core, and to explain why some Orthodox local churches historically included even more texts (Ethiopian, Coptic, Slavonic traditions).

Soteriological implications: This graded model protects against two errors simultaneously — flattening all texts to identical authority, or discarding profitable texts because they lack the highest liturgical rank.

Reflection Questions

  1. Comprehension: What specific evidence does De Young marshal against the claim that the New Testament canon was voted on at the Council of Nicea?

  2. Comprehension: Why is the Jerusalem temple's scroll collection an unreliable basis for reconstructing a supposed first-century "Old Testament canon"?

  3. Theological/Analytical: De Young distinguishes "canonicity" from "deutero-canonicity" as functionally negligible in Orthodox practice, yet notes that some fully canonical books are never read liturgically. What does this suggest about how the Orthodox Church actually measures a text's authority — reading frequency, doctrinal content, historical reception, or something else?

  4. Theological/Analytical: St. Irenaeus recognized Gnostic communities as non-Christian partly because of the different texts they used. How does this connect canon to ecclesial identity, and what does it suggest about the relationship between "what a community reads" and "who a community is"?

  5. Personal/Devotional: How does knowing that nineteen of the twenty-seven New Testament books were already in near-universal use by roughly AD 150 — through ordinary community memory and use, not committee decision — change how you receive the New Testament as trustworthy?

  6. Personal/Devotional: The chapter shows that canon formation took centuries and involved real historical messiness (disputed books, varying local lists, graded pastoral categories). Does this complexity threaten or deepen your trust in the process? Why?

  7. Liturgical/Sacramental: The nine biblical odes at Matins (including the Theotokos's Magnificat) are bound within Old Testament codices without being Old Testament texts. What does this tell you about how liturgical use and canonical status relate — and don't always neatly align — in Orthodox practice?

  8. Liturgical/Sacramental: Given that Revelation is still not read liturgically in most Orthodox parishes despite being fully canonical, how might this shape the way you personally engage that book in private study versus how you experience it (or don't) in corporate worship?
