Bible Study — Psalm 47 (Orthodox Study Bible)

LXX / OSB: Psalm 47 (Septuagint) | Psalm 48 (Hebrew/Masoretic)

Overview

Psalm 47 closes the Korahite trio of the city of God (45, 46, 47), and it is the one that finally walks you through the gates. Psalm 45 stood inside a shaking world and found a quiet river; Psalm 46 watched God go up with a shout; Psalm 47 takes you on a tour. Its superscription in the Greek is fuller than the Hebrew's — "A psalm of an ode for the sons of Korah; for the second day of the week" — assigning it a fixed place in the cycle of temple song, which the Fathers connected to the second day of creation, when the firmament was set and the waters were given a boundary. The psalm moves in four beats: the city is praised and located, rooted and beautiful, on a mountain with a strange name (vv. 2–4); hostile kings arrive, look at it, and fall apart in labor pains without a battle being fought (vv. 5–8); the worshippers testify that what they had been told they have now seen, and turn that seeing into contemplation inside the temple (vv. 9–11); and then, in the psalm's most concrete command, they are sent to walk the walls, count the towers, and report what they find to the next generation (vv. 12–15). It ends not with a fortress but with a shepherd: "He will shepherd us unto the ages." Read straight through, Psalm 47 is the trio's answer to the question the other two raise — if God is in the city, what is the city actually like? — and its answer is that you should go look, and then go tell someone.

Section 1 (vv. 2–4): The City, the Mountain, and the Strange North

OSB Notes

The psalm opens with praise that immediately gets an address: "Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, in His holy mountain." The OSB reads the city throughout as the Church — Zion as type, the Body of Christ as fulfillment (Heb 12:22, "you have come to Mount Zion... the city of the living God") — and the geography as theology rather than surveying. Verse 3 supplies three descriptions that do not obviously belong together: the city is "well-rooted in joy throughout the earth," it is "the mountains of Zion, the sides of the north," and it is "the city of the great King." The middle phrase is the strange one. "The sides of the north" (τὰ πλευρὰ τοῦ βορρᾶ) renders a Hebrew expression naming the mythic mountain of the gods in the surrounding Canaanite world — the divine assembly's seat — and the psalm calmly relocates it to Zion. Whatever the nations thought was happening on the holy mountain of the north is happening here instead, which is the same polemic Psalm 81 (LXX) runs against the divine council. The "great King" of v. 3 is picked up directly by Christ in Matthew 5:35, where Jerusalem is "the city of the great King," and the OSB treats that as the Lord's own commentary on this verse. Verse 4 then names how God is known: "God is known in her palaces when He helps her" — not by inspection of the architecture, but by His action inside it.

Theological Themes

  • The city is located, not abstract. The psalm refuses a purely interior religion. Praise happens somewhere, in a community with walls and towers and a history of being helped.
  • The rival mountain is annexed. By calling Zion "the sides of the north," the psalm does not deny that the nations have a holy mountain; it says they have the wrong one, and takes the name. The claim is exclusive without being dismissive.
  • God is known by what He does. Verse 4 makes divine knowability a function of divine action. The city's residents know Him because they have been helped, which is a knowledge unavailable from outside the walls.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Basil's treatment of mountain imagery in the Psalms consistently turns on stability and elevation together — a mountain is what does not move and what can be seen from far off — and applied here that yields his characteristic pastoral point: the city's security is not a wall the citizens built but the mountain God set it on, and the visible steadiness of the Church is meant to be legible to people standing outside it. He is equally firm that "great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised" is not a statement the psalm leaves as information; the doubled adverb makes praise proportional to greatness, and a small praise for a great God is a species of falsehood. On v. 4 he presses the ascetic edge: God is known when He helps her, so the soul that has never needed help has never known Him, and dependence is not a stage the Christian outgrows but the permanent condition of knowledge.

A Second Father's Reading

Maximus the Confessor, in the Mystagogy, reads the church — building, assembly, and liturgy at once — as an image working on several levels simultaneously: the whole cosmos, the visible and invisible creation, and the individual human person, each mirrored in the same structure. Brought to this psalm, that hermeneutic keeps vv. 2–4 from collapsing into either pure history or pure allegory. The city is Jerusalem, and the Church, and also you: the nave and sanctuary correspond to body and soul, so "God is known in her palaces when He helps her" describes what happens in the assembly and what happens inside a single person receiving grace, without either reading cancelling the other. Maximus's instinct is that the levels are not competing interpretations but the same reality seen at different magnifications — which is exactly how the Fathers use the city of Psalm 47.

Ascetic Movement

The section cultivates tapeinosis (ταπείνωσις) in a specific form: the acknowledgment that one is a resident rather than a founder. It addresses the logismos of spiritual self-sufficiency — the thought that faith is a private arrangement needing no city, no walls, no other people. This is katharsis work at the level of ecclesial belonging, and the purification is of the instinct to relate to God alone.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Verse 4 is the answer to a common catechumen's question about why one must attend at all: God is known in her palaces, in the assembly, through His help given there — so the practice this section calls for is simply presence, week by week, including the weeks when nothing seems to happen. When you enter the church and venerate the icons at the door, let v. 2 be the sentence in your mouth: praise proportioned to greatness, spoken in the place where it belongs. And if the "sides of the north" of v. 3 means anything practically, it means the Church has no need to be defensive about rival holy places — it simply occupies the ground.

LXX Notes

Verse 3 is the psalm's first major divergence. The Hebrew reads that Zion is "beautiful in elevation" (yefeh nof), a comment on the city's appearance; the LXX has εὖ ῥιζῶν ἀγαλλιάματι, "well-rooted in joy" — the translators read the consonants as rootedness rather than height, and the resulting image is agricultural instead of scenic. The Greek city is planted, and planted in gladness, which is why the Fathers can move so easily from this verse to the tree of Psalm 1 and to the Church as God's field. "The sides of the north" is a literal rendering of a Hebrew phrase whose mythological freight (the mountain of the divine assembly) the Greek preserves without softening, leaving the polemic intact for anyone who knows what the phrase meant to Israel's neighbors.

Section 2 (vv. 5–8): The Kings Came, Looked, and Went Into Labor

OSB Notes

Verses 5–7 are a battle narrative with no battle. "For behold, the kings assembled; they came together; they saw her; thus they marveled; they were troubled; they were shaken; trembling laid hold of them; there were labor pains, like a woman giving birth." The verbs run in sequence — assembled, came, saw, marveled, troubled, shaken, trembling, labor — and the turning point is they saw. Nothing is done to them; they look at the city and disintegrate. The OSB reads this against the same historical backdrop that stands behind Psalm 45, the coalition that besieged Jerusalem and withdrew without a fight, and forward to the Church's endurance against powers that outmatch it materially and cannot account for its persistence. The birth-pang image of v. 7 (ὠδῖνες) is the tradition's hinge into eschatology: it is the vocabulary Christ uses for the convulsions preceding the end (Matt 24:8, "the beginning of birth pangs") and Paul uses for the sudden destruction that overtakes those saying "peace and safety" (1 Thess 5:3). Verse 8's "You shall wreck the ships of Tarshish with a violent wind" names the era's maximum technology — the deep-sea merchant fleets — and breaks them with weather.

Theological Themes

  • Defeat by sight. The kings are undone by looking. The psalm's claim is that the city's mere existence is an argument the hostile cannot answer, which sets the pattern for martyrdom as witness rather than as resistance.
  • Birth pangs, not death throes. The image in v. 7 is deliberately ambiguous in a way the Fathers exploit: labor is agony that produces something. The same convulsion that terrifies the kings is, on the other side of history, delivery.
  • The strongest thing available is the thing broken. Ships of Tarshish are the era's projection of power across distance. God does not counter them with better ships.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Chrysostom's reading of a passage like this is pastoral and slightly combative: he wants his congregation to notice that the Church has never won its arguments by matching its opponents' resources, and that every generation is tempted to think this time is different. The kings in v. 6 do not lose a fight; they lose their composure — and Chrysostom's conclusion is that what unsettles the powers is not Christian strength but Christian steadiness, the visible fact of a community that will not stop existing. He turns the point inward before letting it stand: the same is true of the soul under assault by the passions, which are routed less by direct combat than by the settled refusal to be moved, and a person who has stopped negotiating with a temptation has already done to it what the city did to the kings.

A Second Father's Reading

Theodoret of Cyrus supplies the discipline that keeps this section honest. His method throughout the Psalter is to establish the historical occasion first and let the spiritual reading grow out of it rather than replace it — so the kings of v. 5 are real kings with a real campaign, and the psalm is first of all a report of something that happened to a besieged city. Theodoret's restraint matters here precisely because vv. 5–8 invite runaway allegory: read his way, the Church's later experience of hostile power is analogous to Jerusalem's, resting on the claim that the same God acts consistently, rather than on the claim that the kings were never kings. That is also the safeguard against the opposite error — reading the passage as pure political triumphalism — since Theodoret's God delivers a city that had no capacity to deliver itself.

Ascetic Movement

The section addresses deilia (δειλία, cowardice) and the logismoi of intimidation — the thoughts that inventory the opposition's resources and conclude in advance. It cultivates hypomonē (ὑπομονή, endurance) in its corporate form: the endurance of a body rather than of an individual hero. The birth-pang image places the work at the boundary of katharsis and photismos, where suffering starts to be legible as productive rather than merely destructive.

Orthodox Practice Connection

When you are outmatched — at work, in an argument about the faith, in a temptation you have lost to before — the practice this section teaches is not escalation but staying put: pray the Jesus Prayer without answering the thought, and let the passion do what the kings did. Verse 7's labor imagery belongs in the mouth during any protracted trial that has no visible end, and it is worth saying plainly in confession when the sin is the despair underneath, the conviction that the pain is going nowhere. For the ships of Tarshish: the honest question is which technology or capacity you have quietly begun to regard as the thing that actually keeps you safe.

LXX Notes

Verse 8's "with a violent wind" is ἐν πνεύματι βιαίῳ, and the phrase is close enough to Acts 2:2's πνοῆς βιαίας — the "rushing mighty wind" of Pentecost — that the Fathers could hear the two together: the same violent breath that wrecks the fleets of the nations fills the upper room. The Greek word πνεῦμα doing double duty as wind and Spirit is what makes the connection available in Greek and largely invisible in English. In v. 7 the LXX's ὠδῖνες is the standard Greek term for birth pangs and is the exact word behind the New Testament's eschatological usage, so the link to Matthew 24 is lexical rather than thematic. Verse 6's chain of aorists — εἶδον, ἐθαύμασαν, ἐταράχθησαν, ἐσαλεύθησαν — is more clipped in Greek than most English renderings manage, and the staccato is the point: the collapse is fast.

Section 3 (vv. 9–11): As We Heard, So We Saw

OSB Notes

Verse 9 is the psalm's turn from event to testimony: "As we heard, so we saw, in the city of the Lord of hosts, in the city of our God; God established her forever." The OSB treats this as the psalm's epistemology — the community had been told about God's protection by an earlier generation, and has now seen it, so the tradition they received turns out to be verified rather than replaced. This is the verse that makes Psalm 47 a catechetical psalm: hearing comes first, sight follows, and the order is not reversible. Verse 10 then does something unexpected with the confirmation: "We pondered Your mercy, O God, in the midst of Your temple." The response to deliverance is not a victory celebration but contemplation, and it happens inside — the psalm relocates from the walls to the sanctuary. Verse 11 widens again: "According to Your name, O God, so is Your praise to the ends of the earth; Your right hand is full of righteousness." The praise matches the name, and the reach of the praise matches the reach of the earth, which the tradition reads as the missionary consequence of the contemplation in v. 10.

Theological Themes

  • Tradition precedes and survives verification. The community did not discover God independently and then compare notes with their fathers. They were told, and then they saw. Faith here is neither blind nor self-generated.
  • The proper response to rescue is contemplation. Verse 10 is startling in context: the enemy has just collapsed, and the psalm goes indoors to think about mercy. Deliverance is treated as material for reflection rather than as an occasion for a parade.
  • Mercy is what gets pondered. Not the defeat of the kings, not the strength of the walls — the mercy. The psalm names what deserves the community's attention, and it is the attribute least visible in the preceding narrative.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Athanasius's whole argument in the Letter to Marcellinus is that the Psalter does not merely describe the soul's conditions but supplies the words by which they are entered and transformed — and v. 9 is that argument in miniature. The psalm hands a congregation the sentence "as we heard, so we saw," and a person who says it out loud is doing something more than reporting: they are taking their place in a chain of testimony, receiving from those who spoke before and speaking for those who have not yet seen. Athanasius would press v. 10 as the discipline that keeps this from becoming mere enthusiasm — the community goes into the temple to ponder mercy, and the pondering is what converts an experience into knowledge. What is remembered inside determines what is proclaimed to the ends of the earth in v. 11.

A Second Father's Reading

Cyril of Jerusalem's Catechetical Lectures are the natural companion to this section, because Cyril is speaking to exactly the people v. 9 describes: candidates who have heard the faith taught and are approaching the moment of seeing it. His pedagogy assumes the psalm's order — instruction first, mystery afterward — and he is emphatic that the sequence is not a delay to be resented but the shape of how a human being comes to know God at all. His Mystagogical Lectures, delivered only after baptism, embody v. 10's move indoors: there are things that can be explained only to those who have already received them, and the explanation happens in the midst of the temple, not outside it. For a catechumen, Cyril's reading turns v. 9 from a description of someone else's community into a promise about a specific date on the calendar.

Ascetic Movement

This section cultivates theoria (θεωρία) in its properly ecclesial form — contemplation performed inside the temple and inside the received tradition, rather than as private speculation — and the paradosis (παράδοσις) that hands it on. The passion addressed is the modern logismos of originality: the thought that a faith received from others is secondhand and therefore inferior. This is photismos, and the psalm's claim is that illumination arrives through the tradition rather than around it.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Verse 10 is the argument for staying after the Liturgy rather than leaving as soon as the doors close — the pondering happens in the midst of the temple, and there is a reason Orthodox practice fills the building with things to look at slowly. Make v. 9 a specific exercise: name one thing you were taught about God before you believed it, and which you have since seen for yourself; then name one thing you have been told and have not yet seen, and let it stand as told rather than forcing it. For confession: the impulse to treat inherited faith as inferior to discovered faith is worth saying out loud, because it is the exact thought this section is built to dismantle.

LXX Notes

Verse 10's verb is ὑπελάβομεν, which carries the sense of taking up, receiving, or supposing — the Greek is warmer than a bare "we thought on" and closer to the community receiving God's mercy into itself while contemplating it, which is how the Fathers read the line's liturgical setting. The Hebrew "we have thought on your steadfast love (hesed)" is compatible but flatter. In v. 9, both texts read the same, and the Greek's ἠκούσαμεν / εἴδομεν pairing preserves the aural-then-visual order the whole patristic catechetical tradition builds on. Verse 11's "Your right hand is full of righteousness" is δικαιοσύνης πλήρης, and δικαιοσύνη in the LXX regularly carries the sense of covenant faithfulness rather than juridical rectitude alone, which is why the tradition hears mercy and righteousness in this verse as the same hand rather than as competing attributes.

Section 4 (vv. 12–15): Walk Around Zion and Tell the Next Generation

OSB Notes

The psalm ends with a survey and an assignment. "Let Mount Zion be glad; let the daughters of Judah greatly rejoice because of Your judgments, O Lord" (v. 12) — the OSB reads the "daughters of Judah" as the surrounding towns dependent on the city, and in the fulfilled sense the local churches whose gladness derives from the one Church. Then vv. 13–14 issue the psalm's most physical commands: "Encircle Zion and embrace her; describe her towers in detail; set your hearts on her power and observe her palaces, that you may describe them to another generation." This is a walking tour with a purpose — the point of the inspection is the report, and the report is aimed at people not yet born. The tradition reads this as the mandate of catechesis and of the Church's continuous self-description across time: you are told to look carefully at the Church precisely so that you can hand on an accurate account of it. Verse 15 then supplies the psalm's closing surprise. After fourteen verses of towers, palaces, walls, and ramparts, the last image is pastoral: "For He is God, our God, forever and unto ages of ages; He will shepherd us unto the ages." The fortress turns out to be a shepherd, which the OSB connects to John 10 and to Revelation 7:17, where the Lamb shepherds and leads to living waters.

Theological Themes

  • Inspection is commanded, not discouraged. The psalm does not ask for credulity. It says go count the towers — look closely, in detail, at the actual thing — and assumes the examination will survive contact.
  • The next generation is the point. The whole tour is instrumental: "that you may describe them to another generation." A faith held without transmission has not finished obeying this psalm.
  • The city's strength is a Person. Verse 15 dissolves the architecture into shepherding. Everything the walls were doing, He was doing, and He continues doing it "unto the ages."

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Chrysostom is at his most direct on verses of this kind, and his reading of v. 14 falls squarely on the parents and teachers in front of him: the psalm makes the transmission of the faith an obligation with a deadline, since another generation is arriving whether or not anyone has prepared an account for them. He would refuse the excuse that one is unqualified — the psalm does not ask for theologians, it asks for people who have walked around the city and paid attention — and he would press that the reason many cannot describe the towers is simply that they have never looked. On v. 15 his tone would shift entirely: after all the commands, the psalm's last word is that God does the shepherding, so the obligation of v. 14 rests on a promise rather than on the competence of the ones obeying it.

A Second Father's Reading

Irenaeus of Lyons, whose Against Heresies is built on the claim that the apostolic faith has been handed down publicly and can be traced, is the psalm's natural patristic voice at vv. 13–14. His argument against the Gnostics is precisely the psalm's method: the Church's teaching is not a secret deposit available to initiates but a describable thing, held in the open, consistent across the churches, and verifiable by anyone willing to walk the perimeter and count. Irenaeus's confidence that one can point at the succession is the same confidence the psalm shows in commanding an inspection, and it carries the same implication — a Church that could not survive being examined in detail would not be the city this psalm is describing. His rule of faith is, in effect, the report to another generation that v. 14 assigns.

Ascetic Movement

The section addresses acedia in its ecclesial form — the listlessness that cannot be bothered to look closely at what one already belongs to — and cultivates attentiveness turned outward, the praxis of examination and transmission rather than the interior work the earlier sections required. Verse 15's shepherding places the whole psalm's ascetic labor under grace: the movement toward theosis here is corporate and generational, a flock led rather than individuals climbing.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Take vv. 13–14 literally at least once: walk through your parish slowly and look at what is actually there — the icons and who they depict, the feasts on the calendar, what happens in the services you attend on autopilot — with the explicit intention of being able to explain it to someone else. That is what the psalm commands, and it is also the best cure for a faith that has gone vague. Verse 15's "He will shepherd us" belongs in the mouth at the end of any stretch where you have been the one doing the carrying; and if you are preparing for reception into the Church, notice that this psalm makes describing the city to another generation part of the job you are taking on, not an advanced option.

LXX Notes

Verse 15 is the psalm's most consequential divergence. The Greek ends ποιμανεῖ ἡμᾶς εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας — "He will shepherd us unto the ages" — while the Hebrew's final words (al-muth) are obscure and are commonly rendered "He will be our guide even unto death," or attached to the following psalm's superscription as a musical notation. The LXX's shepherd reading gives the psalm a Christological ending the Hebrew does not clearly supply, and it aligns Psalm 47 with Psalm 22's shepherd and with Christ's own claim in John 10 — one of the clearer cases where the Greek preserves a reading the tradition depends on. In v. 13 the verbs are concrete and tactile: κυκλώσατε ("encircle") and περιλάβετε ("embrace," literally take around), so the command is closer to walking the walls with your hands on them than to admiring from a distance. Verse 14's καταδιέλεσθε means to divide up and distribute in detail — the inspection is meant to be itemized, not impressionistic.

Synthesis

This psalm sends you outside to look and then brings you back in to think, and it will not let you skip either. It begins by insisting that praise has an address — a city, a mountain, a community where God is known because He has actually helped it — and then shows you what happens to the powers that come to break it: they look at it and go into labor. But the psalm's center is not the collapse of the kings; it is the sentence the survivors say afterward, as we heard, so we saw. That is the whole shape of a catechumen's life compressed into five words: you are told first, by people who were told before you, and then you are given eyes. And what the psalm does with that verification is the thing most likely to be skipped — it goes indoors and ponders mercy, in the middle of the temple, rather than celebrating in the street. Only then does it send you back out with a tape measure: encircle the city, embrace her, count the towers, itemize the palaces, and describe them to another generation. So Psalm 47 calls you to a faith that can be inspected, received, contemplated, and handed on — and it ends by taking the weight off you entirely. After all the walls and ramparts, the last word is not a fortification. It is a shepherd, and He is leading unto the ages.

Discussion Questions

  • The psalm gives praise an address — "in the city of our God." What is lost when faith is held entirely privately, and what does it cost you to hold it somewhere with other people instead?
  • The hostile kings of vv. 5–7 are undone by looking, not by being fought. What does it mean that the Church's witness has historically worked more by existing than by winning arguments — and where are you tempted to reach for better weapons instead?
  • Verse 7 describes the kings' collapse as labor pains. Why do you think Scripture reaches for the image of childbirth for terror and upheaval, and what does that suggest about trials that seem to be going nowhere?
  • "As we heard, so we saw." Name something you were taught about God before you had any evidence for it, and which you have since seen for yourself. What did the gap between the hearing and the seeing feel like?
  • Verse 10 responds to deliverance by going into the temple to ponder mercy rather than by celebrating. What would it change about your week if the response to good news were contemplation rather than relief?
  • The psalm commands a detailed inspection of the city so that it can be described "to another generation." If you had to describe the Church accurately to someone who knew nothing, what could you actually say — and where would you run out?
