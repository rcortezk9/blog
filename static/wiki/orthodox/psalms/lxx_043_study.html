Bible Study — Psalm 43 (Orthodox Study Bible)

LXX / OSB: Psalm 43 (Septuagint) | Psalm 44 (Hebrew/Masoretic)

Overview

This is a corporate lament of the sons of Korah, superscribed "For the End... for understanding" — a heading the Fathers read as pointing past the historical crisis to Christ, who is the telos toward which the whole Psalter runs. Its movement is unusually stark: a long, confident recital of what God did for the fathers (vv. 2–9), then a sudden and unexplained reversal — "But now You rejected and disgraced us" (v. 10) — followed by a protest of innocence that is unique in the Psalter, since the community insists it has not forgotten God or broken covenant (vv. 18–22). It ends not in resolution but in a cry that God would wake up: "Awake; why do You sleep, O Lord?" (v. 24). St. Paul quotes v. 23 — "For Your sake we are put to death all day long, we are counted as sheep for the slaughter" — in Romans 8:36, which fixes the Church's reading of this Psalm permanently: it is the prayer of the martyrs and of every suffering member of Christ's Body whose affliction is not punishment for sin. The OSB reads the Psalm's unanswered ending as deliberate; the answer is not given inside the Psalm because the answer is the Cross.

Section 1 (vv. 1–9): What Our Fathers Told Us

OSB Notes

The superscription "For the End" (εἰς τὸ τέλος) is read throughout the Psalter as a Christological pointer — the "end" is not the finish of a musical piece but Christ Himself, the end of the law (Rom 10:4) — while "for understanding" marks the Psalm as instruction, not merely complaint. The OSB's reading of vv. 4–7 centers on the Psalm's own explicit theology of salvation: Israel did not take the land "with their sword," and "their arm did not save them"; it was God's right hand, His arm, and "the light of Your countenance." That phrase — the light of God's face — is read as the uncreated light of God's favor, the same light the Church names in the divine energies.

Theological Themes

  • Salvation is received, not achieved. The Psalm states it twice for emphasis (vv. 4, 7): the conquest was God's arm, and the singer explicitly refuses to hope in his own bow. The whole logic of synergy in Orthodox soteriology stands on this side of the line — cooperation, never self-rescue.
  • Faith transmitted by testimony. "We heard with our ears; our fathers proclaimed it to us" (v. 2). The community's knowledge of God is inherited, handed down — Tradition operating in the Psalm's own bones before it is a theological term.
  • Praise before the reversal. vv. 5–9 are pure confidence, and they are placed first. The lament that follows is spoken by a soul that has already praised God "all day long," not by one who never knew Him.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Chrysostom's characteristic move with this opening is to press on the phrase "our fathers proclaimed it to us": the singers of this Psalm have no personal memory of the Red Sea or the conquest, and they are afflicted now — yet the first thing they do in their distress is rehearse a deliverance they never saw. The Fathers do not treat this as nostalgia but as the soul's basic discipline in trial: before you say anything about your own affliction, you say what God has already done, because a soul that begins with its own wound cannot see straight. Athanasius's counsel in the Letter to Marcellinus runs parallel — the Psalter gives you words to speak, so that speaking them forms the soul that speaks.

Ascetic Movement

This section works against the passion of self-reliance and its root, philautia (φιλαυτία, self-love), by putting a confession into your mouth that you cannot save yourself: "I will not hope in my bow." It belongs to katharsis, the purgative stage — the stripping away of the illusion that one's own arm is the operative power in one's own life, which is precisely the illusion that makes later suffering unbearable.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Pray vv. 2–4 when you are tempted to take credit for a deliverance — a job that came through, a crisis that passed, a passion that quieted — and let the Psalm correct the attribution. This is also the interior work of the Eucharistic anaphora, where the priest recites what God has done before asking anything at all; bringing that same order into private prayer (recital first, petition second) is a small, concrete imitation of the Liturgy's own grammar.

LXX Notes

"For the End" translates the LXX's εἰς τὸ τέλος where the Hebrew has a musical direction to the choirmaster; the Greek rendering is what opens the whole Christological reading of this and dozens of other superscriptions, and it is why the Orthodox Psalter hears Christ in headings that a Hebrew-based translation renders as liturgical stage directions. "For understanding" (συνέσεως) similarly renders the Hebrew maskil, turning a genre label into a claim about what the Psalm is for.

Section 2 (vv. 10–17): But Now You Rejected Us

OSB Notes

The reversal in v. 10 is grammatically abrupt and theologically shocking: every verb of defeat has God as its subject. He rejected, He disgraced, He turned us back, He gave us as sheep to be eaten, He scattered us. The OSB does not soften this into "God permitted"; the Psalm insists on divine agency, and the Church prays it that way. The image of being "given as sheep to be eaten" (v. 12) prepares directly for v. 23's "sheep for the slaughter," which Paul will apply to the martyrs.

Theological Themes

  • Lament addressed to God, not about Him. The Psalm's complaint is spoken in the second person throughout — You rejected, You scattered. This is not doubt but prayer of an unusually direct kind; the very act of accusation presupposes the covenant it protests.
  • Shame as a distinct affliction. vv. 14–17 dwell not on physical loss but on reproach, derision, being made "a byword" — the specific wound of humiliation before others, which the Psalm treats as worth naming to God in its own right.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Basil the Great's instinct with passages that attribute affliction directly to God is to distinguish between what God does and what God permits for a purpose — but he refuses to use that distinction to make the sufferer's cry less honest. The Fathers read this section as divine pedagogy: God withdraws the sense of His help, not His help, and the withdrawal itself is the instrument by which a people who "shall push back our enemies" (v. 6) learn they were never the ones pushing. Chrysostom presses the point that the shame in vv. 14–17 is permitted precisely because reputation is the last idol a faithful person surrenders.

Ascetic Movement

This is the territory of what the Fathers call the withdrawal of grace — the experience of dryness and abandonment that tests whether one loved God or loved God's consolations. The ascetic response is hypomonē (ὑπομονή, patient endurance), not explanation; the Psalm models staying in the room with God through the silence rather than concluding from the silence that no one is there. It is also a direct assault on vainglory: to be made "a shaking of the head among the peoples" (v. 15) is the death of one's public self.

Orthodox Practice Connection

When prayer has gone dry and God seems absent, this section gives you permission to say so to Him in the strongest terms the Psalter allows — which is the honest content to bring to your spiritual father rather than to bury. The Jesus Prayer prayed in this state, without felt consolation, is exactly the practice this Psalm is training: continuing to address the One who seems to have turned away.

LXX Notes

v. 13, "You bound Your people without honor, and there was no abundance given to ransom them," follows the LXX's reading of a people sold cheaply, with no ransom price gained; the Greek makes the humiliation the point — not that God sold them for little profit, but that they were reckoned as worth nothing. The martyrological reading of the Psalm depends on this: those slaughtered "for Your sake" are, in the world's ledger, worthless.

Section 3 (vv. 18–23): We Did Not Forget You

OSB Notes

Here the Psalm does what almost no other lament in the Psalter does: it denies that the suffering is deserved. "All these things came upon us, but we have not forgotten You, and we did not act unjustly with Your covenant" (v. 18). The OSB flags v. 23 as the verse St. Paul quotes in Romans 8:36 to describe the Church's condition between the Resurrection and the End — suffering that cannot separate us from the love of Christ precisely because it is endured for His sake. v. 22, "He knows the secrets of the heart," is the Psalm's own guarantee that its protest of innocence is not bravado: the community submits its claim to the one Judge who reads the kardia.

Theological Themes

  • Innocent suffering as a real category. Not all affliction is chastisement. The Psalm establishes, inside the canon, that a faithful community can suffer because of its fidelity — which is the theological ground the Church stands on when it honors martyrs rather than pitying them.
  • The heart as the place of verdict. "God searches this out... He knows the secrets of the heart" (v. 22). Innocence is not a matter of public record but of what God sees in the kardia — the same standard by which hidden faithfulness is real even when unrecognized.
  • Martyrdom as the Psalm's true subject. Read through Romans 8:36, "sheep for the slaughter" is not a metaphor for bad fortune but a description of the Church's normal condition in a hostile world.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

The Fathers read v. 23 as the voice of the martyrs and, before them, of Christ — the innocent one slain "all day long" in His members. Chrysostom's repeated point about the martyrs is that their glory lies not in the manner of their death but in the daily athlēsis (ἄθλησις, the contest) that preceded it: a person who has not learned to endure small dishonors will not stand at the great one. This is why the Psalm's protest of innocence is not self-righteousness — the speakers do not claim to be sinless, only that this particular affliction is not the wage of infidelity, and they submit even that claim to the God who searches hearts.

Ascetic Movement

The virtue here is hypomonē matured into athlēsis: endurance that has become contest, suffering consciously offered rather than merely absorbed. The passion it addresses is the logismos — the intrusive thought — that says this is happening because God has judged you, a thought that drives the soul toward despondency (acedia) rather than toward God. Nepsis (νῆψις, watchfulness) is the tool: catching that logismos early, testing it against what God actually knows of your heart, and refusing to let a false verdict masquerade as humility.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Bring the distinction this section makes into Confession: name the sins that are actually yours, and refuse to confess as sin the suffering that is not your fault — a habit that sounds obvious and is, in practice, very hard for the scrupulous. Praying v. 22, "He knows the secrets of the heart," before the Eucharist is also a fitting preparation, since it asks God to judge you by what is truly there rather than by what you or others perform.

LXX Notes

v. 19's LXX reading, "Our heart did not draw back, yet You turned our steps from Your way," keeps the two clauses in tension: the people's kardia held firm while God Himself redirected their steps — a sharper statement of divine agency than translations that render the second clause as the people straying. Paul quotes v. 23 in Romans 8:36 in its Septuagint form, which is why the Church's martyrological reading of this Psalm is not an imposition on the text but an apostolic use of the very Greek the Orthodox Psalter preserves.

Section 4 (vv. 24–27): Awake, Why Do You Sleep?

OSB Notes

The Psalm ends in imperatives, not resolution: "Awake... Arise... help us... redeem us for Your name's sake." The OSB reads "Why do You sleep, O Lord?" as anthropomorphic speech that the Church nonetheless prays literally, and Christian reading has always heard behind it Christ asleep in the stern of the boat while the disciples cry, "Teacher, do You not care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38) — a sleep that was neither absence nor indifference. v. 27's final ground of appeal is not the community's innocence, which it has just spent six verses establishing, but God's own name: redeem us for Your name's sake.

Theological Themes

  • The unanswered ending. No deliverance is narrated. The Psalm closes mid-cry, and the Church keeps it that way — the answer to this Psalm is given outside it, in the Cross and the empty tomb.
  • God's name, not our merit, as the final appeal. Even the innocent do not finally plead innocence; they plead who God is. This is the same logic by which every Orthodox prayer ends in doxology rather than in a case.
  • Humbled to the dust. v. 26 — "our soul was humbled to the dust, and our belly was joined to the ground" — is the physical posture of prostration read as the soul's true condition.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

The Fathers hear in "Awake; why do You sleep?" not an accusation God resents but a cry He invites, and they connect it to the sleeping Christ in the boat: the Lord's apparent sleep is the test by which faith is revealed, and He wakes at the cry. Chrysostom reads v. 26's "our belly was joined to the ground" with characteristic bluntness as the soul dragged earthward by what it clings to — the image of a body pressed flat becoming a diagnosis of a heart pressed flat against material things. The Fathers' consistent counsel on such an ending is that the Psalm gives you a prayer for a state you cannot yet pray your way out of, and that this is itself a mercy.

Ascetic Movement

This is tapeinosis (ταπείνωσις, humility) at its literal root — being brought low, to the dust — and katanyxis (κατάνυξις, compunction), the piercing of the heart that makes a cry possible at all. It marks the transition point in the ascetic journey where katharsis has done its work: the soul has nothing left to offer but God's own name, which is exactly the emptiness in which photismos (illumination) becomes possible.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Pray this section with your body — a prostration at "our soul was humbled to the dust" makes the Psalm's own posture your posture, which is why the Church prostrates rather than merely reflecting during Great Lent. And when you have run out of arguments for why God should help you, v. 27 gives you the one that never runs out: "redeem us for Your name's sake" — the same appeal that stands behind every ending of the Jesus Prayer in "have mercy on me, a sinner."

LXX Notes

v. 26's second clause, "our belly was joined to the ground" (ἐκολλήθη εἰς γῆν ἡ γαστὴρ ἡμῶν), is more concrete in the Greek than most Hebrew-based renderings ("our body clings to the ground"), and the Fathers' ascetic reading — the gastēr, the appetitive belly, glued to earth — depends on that concreteness. v. 24's "Awake; why do You sleep, O Lord?" is preserved without softening in the LXX, and the Church prays it unedited.

Synthesis

This Psalm calls you to suffer without lying — neither pretending the affliction is deserved when it is not, nor pretending God has nothing to do with it. It begins by putting a confession in your mouth that your own arm never saved you, moves through a reversal in which every verb of defeat has God as its subject, and then does the thing no other lament dares: it holds its ground and says we did not forget You, submitting even that claim to the One who reads the secrets of the heart. St. Paul's use of v. 23 in Romans 8:36 tells you where the Church has always stood in this Psalm — with the martyrs, counted as sheep for the slaughter, and not separated from the love of Christ by one inch of it. And its ending, which refuses to resolve, is the point: you are given the words for a state you cannot yet pray your way out of, and the last thing you are given to say is not your innocence but His name. The sleeping Lord in the boat wakes; the Psalm simply does not tell you when.

Discussion Questions

  • The Psalm's opening confidence rests on testimony the singers never witnessed themselves ("our fathers proclaimed it to us"). What does it change to begin a lament by rehearsing what God did for someone else?
  • Every verb of defeat in vv. 10–17 has God as its subject. Why do you think the Church prays this without softening it to "God permitted," and what would be lost if it did?
  • This is one of the only laments in the Psalter that denies the suffering is deserved. Where in your own life have you assumed affliction was punishment when the Psalm would not let you assume that?
  • Paul quotes v. 23 in Romans 8:36 to describe the ordinary condition of the Church. What does it mean to read "sheep for the slaughter" as normal rather than exceptional?
  • "He knows the secrets of the heart" (v. 22) is offered as the guarantee of the community's innocence, not as a threat. When is that verse frightening to you, and when is it a comfort?
  • The Psalm ends with no answer, only "Awake; why do You sleep?" Why might the Church keep a prayer that doesn't resolve, and what does it give you that a resolved Psalm could not?
