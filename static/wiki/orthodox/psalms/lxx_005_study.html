Bible Study — Psalm 5 (Orthodox Study Bible)

LXX / OSB: Psalm 5 (Septuagint) | Psalm 5 (Hebrew/Masoretic)

Overview

Psalm 5 carries the superscription "For the End, for her who inherits, a Psalm of David" — in the Septuagint this heading is counted as verse 1, so the body of the psalm runs vv. 2–13 where Hebrew-based translations number them 1–12. No historical occasion is named. The psalm is a morning prayer, spoken by a man about to go up to worship while his enemies are actively working lies and violence around him, and its movement is fourfold: a cry lifted at daybreak, a meditation on the kind of God who is being prayed to (one who cannot host evil), a resolve to enter His house anyway in reverent fear, and a closing contrast between the deceitful who will fall by their own scheming and the righteous who are covered like a shield by God's favor. Where Psalm 4 closes the day and rests in sleep, Psalm 5 opens the day and rests in worship — the Church has long read the two psalms as morning-and-evening companions, cries offered at the two hinges of the day. The LXX superscription's "her who inherits" (τῆς κληρονομούσης) is itself a matter the Fathers took up directly, reading it as pointing past David's original audience to the Church, the one who now inherits the promises the psalm prays over.

Section 1 (vv. 2–4): The Morning Cry

OSB Notes

The psalm opens with three rising petitions — "Give ear to my words, O Lord, understand my cry. Attend to the voice of my prayer, my King and my God, for to You will I pray, O Lord" — each verb asking God to move closer to the words about to be spoken: hear, understand, attend. The OSB highlights the address "my King and my God" as a claim of personal allegiance made before the request itself, echoing the pattern already seen in Psalm 4 of establishing relationship before petition. Verse 4 then fixes the time: "In the morning You shall hear my voice; in the morning I will stand before You, and You shall look upon me." The repetition of "in the morning" is deliberate — this is not a general statement about prayer but a specific claim about the first hour of the day.

Theological Themes

  • Prayer as an ordered approach, not a single act: three distinct verbs (hear, understand, attend) describe stages of God's attention being sought before the actual request is named.
  • Kingship claimed before help is asked: "my King and my God" establishes covenant loyalty as the ground on which the prayer stands.
  • The morning as the appointed hour of watching: standing before God "in the morning" is presented as a discipline, not an accident of timing.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

St. Athanasius, in his Letter to Marcellinus, commends this psalm to anyone who rises early with enemies still working against him and needs words for the very first thought of the day; the psalm, he teaches, gives the soul language for facing the day's difficulty before the difficulty has even begun. St. John Chrysostom draws attention to the double "in the morning" and teaches that David is not merely praying early but watching early — standing at his post the way a sentry stands, so that the mind belongs to God before the world has had the chance to claim it first. Chrysostom treats this as the pattern for every Christian's day: whoever speaks to the world before speaking to God has already lost the day's first ground.

Ascetic Movement

This section forms the practice the Fathers call watchfulness (nepsis) turned toward the beginning of the day itself — the discipline of giving the mind's first clear hour to God rather than to appetite, anxiety, or the day's demands. The threefold approach of vv. 2–3 (hear, understand, attend) mirrors the ascetic movement of first collecting the scattered mind, then presenting it, before it is fit to ask for anything. This is the entry point of purification each day repeats: the heart cannot be watchful at noon if it was not first stood at attention at dawn.

Orthodox Practice Connection

This is the plain scriptural root of the Church's Morning Prayers (Matins and the household Trisagion Prayers), which are structured, like this psalm, to establish relationship and praise before any petition is voiced. Practically, this section forms the rule of praying before speaking to anyone or looking at anything else — the phone, the news, the day's first obligation — so that the "in the morning" of v. 4 is kept literally rather than only sentimentally. Where the mind resists this discipline, the verse itself becomes the thing to pray: "in the morning I will stand before You," said as a resolve rather than a report.

LXX Notes

The superscription "for her who inherits" (ὑπὲρ τῆς κληρονομούσης) has no clear parallel in the Hebrew, which most translations render "for the flutes" or "with wind instruments" — a musical direction describing an instrument, not a person. The Greek instead names a feminine subject who inherits, and the Fathers read this as the Church, the bride who receives the inheritance promised through Christ; the whole psalm is therefore prayed, on this reading, in the voice of the Church rising to intercede at daybreak. This is one of the clearer instances in the early psalms where the LXX superscription opens an ecclesial reading that the Hebrew musical rubric does not carry.

Section 2 (vv. 5–7): The Holiness That Cannot Host Evil

OSB Notes

The psalm turns from address to meditation on the nature of the God being addressed: "For You are not a God who wants iniquity; nor shall the evil dwell with You. Nor shall the transgressors continue in Your sight; You hate all who work lawlessness. You shall destroy all who speak a lie; the Lord abhors a bloodthirsty and deceitful man." The OSB notes the escalating specificity — iniquity in general, then evil that cannot lodge with God, then transgressors who cannot remain in His sight, then the particular sins of lying and bloodshed named by name. This is not stated as a threat aimed outward so much as a description of the kind of house the psalmist is about to ask to enter: one from which evil is structurally excluded, not merely disapproved of.

Theological Themes

  • Holiness as incompatibility, not merely disapproval: evil "does not dwell" with God — the language is spatial, describing what cannot coexist, not simply what displeases.
  • Escalation from category to particulars: the passage moves from "iniquity" in the abstract to named sins (lying, bloodshed), grounding the general claim in concrete cases.
  • God's hatred as a real predicate: the psalm does not soften "the Lord abhors" into something more comfortable — it names divine aversion plainly.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Chrysostom reads this section as the necessary hinge of the psalm: before the psalmist asks to enter God's house in the next section, he first states plainly what kind of house it is, so that the request to enter is not made carelessly. Coming near to this God, Chrysostom teaches, is not a neutral act; it is coming near to a holiness that has already excluded, by its nature, everything the psalmist is praying to be delivered from. Athanasius reads the specificity of "bloodthirsty and deceitful" as the psalm's ordinary use throughout the Psalter — a general statement of God's nature, given so that anyone facing violence or slander in particular may take up these exact words as descriptions of what they are contending against.

Ascetic Movement

This section forms sober self-examination before approaching God — not a general sense of unworthiness, but a specific reckoning with which named sins would exclude a person from the house about to be entered. This is the beginning of katharsis: seeing clearly, before worship, what cannot come in with you. The passions the psalm names directly — lying and bloodthirst — are the two the ascetic tradition treats as most corrosive to communion, because both destroy trust between persons, which is the fabric prayer and worship depend on.

Orthodox Practice Connection

This is the section to bring honestly to the Prayers Before Communion and to Confession: not a vague sense of falling short, but the specific question the psalm poses — is there lying or violence, even of the smaller domestic kind (gossip, exaggeration, contempt), that I am carrying toward the altar? The Orthodox practice of preparing for the Eucharist with fasting and confession exists precisely so that this section is not merely read but acted on before the next section's resolve to enter is made.

LXX Notes

No significant textual variation in this section; the Hebrew and Greek align closely on the content, though the LXX's "the Lord abhors" (βδελύξεται) carries a stronger physical connotation of loathing than some English renderings of the underlying Hebrew convey — the same root used elsewhere in the LXX for cultic abomination, sharpening the sense that these sins are not merely displeasing but ritually and morally repugnant to God.

Section 3 (vv. 8–10): Entering the House in Fear, and the Straight Path

OSB Notes

Against the holiness just described, the psalmist does not withdraw but resolves to approach: "But as for me, in the multitude of Your mercy I will enter Your house; I will worship toward Your holy temple in fear of You." The OSB notes the pivot word "but" — the psalmist has no standing of his own after the previous section's description of what God excludes, so entry is grounded entirely in "the multitude of Your mercy," not in any claim to belong there by right. Verse 9 turns the prayer toward guidance: "Lead me, O Lord, in Your righteousness because of my enemies; make my way straight before You." Verse 10 names the danger driving that request: "For there is no truth in their mouth; their heart is vain; their throat is an open sepulcher; with their tongues they have spoken deceitfully."

Theological Themes

  • Mercy, not merit, as the ground of entry: the psalmist's access to worship is explicitly "in the multitude of Your mercy," directly following a section that ruled out any claim of his own.
  • Fear as the right posture of nearness: "worship in fear of You" pairs boldness of approach with reverence, not casualness.
  • Speech as the diagnostic organ of the wicked: the enemies are characterized entirely by their mouths — no truth, a vain heart, an open sepulcher for a throat.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Chrysostom teaches that v. 8 is the psalm's turning hinge and its whole theology of approach in miniature: no one enters God's house on the strength of having avoided the sins just named, but only "in the multitude of Your mercy" — a phrase Chrysostom says should be worn on the heart by anyone who feels too unworthy to pray. St. Basil the Great, commenting on the image of the "open sepulcher," teaches that speech which flatters or deceives functions exactly like a grave: it looks ordinary from outside while what it actually delivers is death, and the psalm's warning is aimed as much at guarding one's own tongue as at naming the enemy's.

Ascetic Movement

This section carries the ascetic movement from katharsis toward active guidance — having examined what cannot enter God's house, the soul now asks not merely to be forgiven but to be led, because knowing what is wrong is not the same as being able to walk straight. "Make my way straight before You" is a plea against self-guided correction, the recognition that the passions named in the previous section will reassert themselves without God actively directing the path. The image of the tongue as an open sepulcher also trains watchfulness over one's own speech, since the tradition applies psalms about enemies reflexively, to the logismoi that speak falsely inside the one praying.

Orthodox Practice Connection

Verse 8's "in the multitude of Your mercy I will enter" is close kin to the prayers said on approaching the chalice, where the communicant likewise claims no worthiness of their own and enters solely on mercy — this verse is worth praying literally on the walk into the church building. "Lead me, O Lord, in Your righteousness" is a fitting request to carry into any day where a specific temptation or a difficult person is expected, asking not for the trial's removal but for a straight path through it. The warning about the "open sepulcher" throat belongs in an examination of conscience focused specifically on speech — flattery, gossip, and words that sound kind but carry harm.

LXX Notes

"I will worship toward Your holy temple" translates a Greek phrase (προσκυνήσω πρὸς ναὸν ἅγιόν σου) that the Hebrew shares closely, though the Hebrew's parallel term is more often rendered "house" throughout, with "temple" specifically named; the LXX's consistent vocabulary here reinforces the psalm's spatial imagery of drawing near to a holy dwelling. St. Paul later draws directly on the LXX language of v. 9 in Romans 3:13 — "their throat is an open grave... the poison of asps is under their lips" — folding this verse into his indictment of universal human sinfulness, a use that depends on the LXX phrasing rather than paraphrasing the Hebrew independently.

Section 4 (vv. 11–13): Judgment on the Deceitful, Joy for the Righteous

OSB Notes

The psalm closes with a plea for judgment paired with a blessing: "Judge them, O God; let them fall by their own counsels; according to the multitude of their ungodliness, cast them out, for they have provoked You, O Lord. But let all who hope in You be glad; they shall ever rejoice, and You will dwell among them; and all who love Your name shall boast in You. For You will bless the righteous, O Lord; You have crowned us with the shield of Your favor." The OSB notes the request is not for direct destruction but that the wicked "fall by their own counsels" — their downfall is their own scheming turned back on itself, not an arbitrary act of divine violence. The psalm's last image, being "crowned... with the shield of Your favor," binds two pictures together: a crown (honor, elevation) and a shield (protection, covering) as a single gift.

Theological Themes

  • Self-defeating evil: the prayer asks that the wicked "fall by their own counsels" — justice as the natural unraveling of deceit under its own weight, not an imposed catastrophe.
  • God's dwelling as the reward of hope: "You will dwell among them" makes God's presence, not merely His protection, the content of the blessing given to those who hope in Him.
  • Favor pictured as both crown and shield: honor and defense are given as one gift, not two separate rewards.

Patristic & Ascetic Formation

The Father's Reading

Chrysostom reads "let them fall by their own counsels" as a statement about the built-in nature of deceit rather than a vengeful request — lies and violence, left long enough, undo the one who uses them, and the psalmist is asking only that God not intervene to rescue his enemies from the natural end of their own schemes. Athanasius reads the psalm's closing turn to communal joy — "let all who hope in You be glad... and You will dwell among them" — as characteristic of the Psalter's movement generally: prayer that begins in the single voice of one man beset by enemies regularly widens, by its end, into the joy of the whole assembly, so that the individual's morning cry becomes, by the last verse, the Church's shared confidence.

Ascetic Movement

This section forms the ascetic discipline of releasing judgment to God rather than executing it oneself — asking that deceit be exposed and undone without taking that undoing into one's own hands. This is a direct exercise against the passion of resentment: naming the wrong plainly to God, in strong language, and then setting it down rather than carrying it forward as a private grievance. The psalm's final picture of being crowned and shielded by favor is the fruit that meets the one who has done this — not triumph over the enemy personally secured, but rest found in God's covering instead.

Orthodox Practice Connection

"Judge them, O God" belongs in prayer exactly as the psalm states it — spoken to God about a specific injury, and then left there, rather than rehearsed toward the person involved; this is the same discipline Psalm 4 taught about anger examined before sleep, now applied to the day's opening prayer instead. The closing verse's crown-and-shield image is fitting language for the end of Morning Prayers or before leaving the house for the day, asking to be covered rather than merely protected. Where a specific person's dishonesty or hostility is weighing on someone, this whole final section — read slowly, aloud — is a direct and licit way to bring that person before God without sinning against them in the process.

LXX Notes

"You have crowned us with the shield of Your favor" translates a Greek text that fuses the images of crowning and shielding into a single act (ἐστεφάνωσας ἡμᾶς); some Hebrew-based translations instead read "You surround him with favor as with a shield," keeping the two images of crown and shield more separate or rendering only the shield explicitly. The Greek's tighter fusion — favor given as both crown and covering in one motion — is the reading that shaped the psalm's liturgical use as a psalm of confident blessing rather than only of protection.

Synthesis

Psalm 5 calls you to give God your day's first clear hour, not the leftover minutes after the world has already had its say. It does not let you enter that morning prayer carelessly — it makes you look first at exactly what kind of holiness you are approaching, a holiness that cannot host evil, before it lets you say, with everything you are, "but as for me, in the multitude of Your mercy I will enter Your house." That is the psalm's whole shape: cry lifted at dawn, holiness reckoned with honestly, entry made on mercy alone and guided by a path you did not choose for yourself, and a closing confidence that hands the deceitful over to their own undoing while resting your own hope in the fact that God will dwell among those who love His name. Where Psalm 4 taught you to end the day by piercing your heart and handing it over before sleep, Psalm 5 teaches you to begin the day by presenting that same heart before you have had the chance to defend or excuse it. The Fathers heard the whole psalm prayed in the voice of the Church, "she who inherits," rising each morning to intercede — which means this is never only your private morning prayer; it is the prayer of the whole Body offered new at every daybreak. The last word is not judgment but a crown: you are not sent into the day merely defended, but favored, covered like a shield, and free to enter.

Discussion Questions

  • The psalm opens with three verbs asking for God's attention (hear, understand, attend) before any request is made. What difference does it make to name what you want from prayer itself before naming what you want prayer to accomplish?
  • Verses 5–7 describe a holiness that structurally cannot host evil — not just disapproval, but incompatibility. How does reckoning with that honestly change the way you approach worship, rather than approaching it casually?
  • Verse 8 pivots entirely on "in the multitude of Your mercy," offered with no claim to personal worthiness. Where in your own life do you find it hardest to approach God on mercy alone rather than on some sense of having earned it?
  • The enemies in v. 10 are diagnosed entirely by their speech — "their throat is an open sepulcher." What makes deceptive speech more dangerous, in the psalm's own logic, than open hostility?
  • The psalm asks that the wicked "fall by their own counsels" rather than asking God to strike them down directly. What does it look like, practically, to hand a specific grievance to God this way rather than carrying it yourself?
  • The psalm closes with a crown and a shield given together — honor and protection as one gift. What would it change about how you enter today if you believed you were already covered rather than merely being tested?
