Summary

1 Corinthians 11 divides into two unified sections — head coverings (vv. 2–16) and the Lord's Supper (vv. 17–34) — both concerned with how the assembled Church conducts itself in worship. The Orthodox lens reads both sections within liturgical theology and patristic exegesis rather than the cultural-propriety or subordination frameworks common in Western commentary. The Eucharistic section (vv. 17–34) contains the earliest written account of the institution narrative and is foundational to Orthodox sacramental theology.

Key Points

  • The glory argument (v. 7): Man is "image and glory of God" and goes uncovered; woman is "glory of man" and is veiled — not from shame but because human glory must be veiled before divine glory in worship. The covering is a liturgical act of reverence, paralleling the cherubim covering their faces before the throne (Isa 6).
  • The heavenly taxis: Orthodox liturgical theology reads "because of the angels" (v. 10) as indicating angelic real presence in the assembly. Worship order participates in the cosmic liturgical order, not merely cultural propriety.
  • The bishop's mitre is a crown, not a veil: The mitre signifies the bishop's High Priestly and royal office — it functions typologically, not in the honor-shame register Paul addresses. Many Orthodox traditions have the bishop remove the mitre at the Gospel reading, preserving 1 Cor 11's logic at the theologically decisive moment.
  • Anamnesis (v. 24–25): "In remembrance of me" (anamnesis) is never mere recollection in Orthodox theology — it is a making-present of the one sacrifice. The Liturgy enters Calvary; it does not look back at it.
  • Real presence (metabole): Bread and wine truly become the Body and Blood of Christ. Orthodoxy affirms this without the scholastic term transubstantiation, using metabole (transformation) instead.
  • "Discerning the body" holds two meanings: (1) recognizing Christ's real presence in the gifts — basis for Eucharistic discipline; (2) recognizing Christ's Body in the gathered poor — Chrysostom applies this to his Antiochene congregation directly. Both failures are failures of discernment; Orthodox commentary holds them together.
  • Eucharistic preparation from v. 28: The "examine yourself" mandate grounds the Orthodox rule: Eucharistic fast, Canon of Preparation, recent confession, vigilance of life. Unworthy reception is an ontological danger, not merely a spiritual risk.
  • The Eucharist constitutes the Church: Schmemann's thesis — the Eucharist is not something the Church does but what the Church is. The Corinthian class divisions were a denial of the Church's own being.

Details

Part 1: Head Coverings (vv. 2–16)

The kephalē chain: God → Christ → man → woman. Immediately balanced in vv. 11–12: "In the Lord, woman is not independent of man, nor man of woman." This is a theological ordering, not an ontological hierarchy.

The glory argument: The core insight of Chrysostom's Homily 26. In the presence of God, what is creaturely must yield to what is divine. Man images God's glory directly; woman images man's glory. Both kinds of glory are real — but human glory in all forms is veiled in the sanctuary so divine glory alone shines. Chrysostom reads the woman's veil as analogous to the iconostasis: the veil between nave and sanctuary hides human glory so that divine glory can be approached.

Heavenly worship frame: The Liturgy is not a human gathering God attends — it is the Church's participation in the worship before the throne already happening. The Cherubikon: "We who mystically represent the cherubim…" Angels are literally present. Order in the assembly, including the covering of women, aligns with the taxis of heavenly worship.

Counter-cultural in both directions: Paul's instruction to men runs against Jewish synagogue practice (men pray with tallit) and Roman religious practice (men covered their heads during sacrifice — velatio capitis). The uncovered male head in Christian worship was a deliberate marker of Christian distinctiveness.

The bishop's mitre resolved: The mitre is not a "head covering" in Paul's sense. It is a vestment of office layering three symbols: (a) crown of thorns / royal suffering, (b) High Priestly office, (c) Byzantine imperial crown. The bishop operates in a typological register, not the cultural-honor register Paul addresses. The mitre removal at the Gospel reading is the tradition preserving Paul's logic at the decisive moment.

Practice in Orthodoxy: Traditional Orthodox practice maintains head coverings for women in church — not as social convention but as liturgical vesture, signaling entry into sacred space. The same impulse as removing shoes on holy ground.

Part 2: The Lord's Supper (vv. 17–34)

The problem at Corinth: The Eucharist was held within a communal agape meal. Wealthy members arrived early, ate their own food, got drunk; slaves arrived later and went hungry. Paul's rebuke: this is not the Lord's Supper but your own supper. The Corinthians were replicating Greco-Roman dining hierarchies at the table where all hierarchies are abolished.

Anamnesis: The Anaphora of St. John Chrysostom enacts the full content of anamnesis: "Remembering therefore this saving commandment and all those things which have come to pass for us — the Cross, the Tomb, the Resurrection on the third day, the Ascension into heaven, the Sitting at the right hand, and the second and glorious Coming…" The one sacrifice is made present, not repeated.

Real presence: St. Cyril of Jerusalem (Mystagogical Catecheses IV): "Do not regard the Bread and the Cup as simply that; for they are, according to the Master's declaration, the Body and Blood of Christ. Even though sense suggests the contrary, let faith be your foundation."

Discerning the body (v. 29): Orthodox commentary holds both readings together:

  • Recognizing Christ's real presence in the Eucharist → closed communion, Eucharistic discipline
  • Recognizing Christ's Body in the assembled poor → Chrysostom is fierce, applying this to his Antiochene congregation directly

Eucharistic preparation (v. 28): "Let a person examine himself." Orthodox preparation: Eucharistic fast from midnight, Canon of Preparation, prayers of Chrysostom and Basil, recent confession. The Liturgy itself warns: Ta hagia tois hagiois — "Holy Things for the Holy" — and the people respond: "One is Holy, One is Lord, Jesus Christ." Unworthy reception brings real ontological consequence.

The Eucharist constitutes the Church: Fr. Alexander Schmemann (For the Life of the World) — the Eucharist is not merely something the Church does; it is what the Church is. The Corinthian divisions were not social rudeness but a denial of the Church's being. This is why schism is the gravest ecclesiological wound and why the Eucharist is not open to non-Orthodox: cup-sharing is not symbolic fellowship but ontological unity.
