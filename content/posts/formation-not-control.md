You're holding the verse like it's a verdict. *"Honor your father and your mother," and, "Whoever curses father or mother, let him be put to death."* Christ says it plainly in Mark 7:10, quoting Exodus straight at the Pharisees, and somewhere in you it lands on the wound instead of on them. You read it in the middle of the argument, or right after it, when your chest is still tight, and it doesn't feel like teaching. It feels like a sentence being read against you.

I'm writing from a little further down the road, after I let that verse sit for a long time instead of using it as a weapon against myself or anyone else. I want to walk it with you the way I finally walked it — because the verse is doing something much more specific, and much less crushing, than it sounds on first read.

## What the verse is actually quoting

Christ isn't inventing a new law in Mark 7. He's citing two things at once: Exodus 20:12, the fifth commandment, *"Honor your father and your mother,"* and then Exodus 21:17 and Leviticus 20:9, which prescribe death for a child who curses a parent. He quotes both together, on purpose, because the Pharisees he's confronting had found a legal loophole — *korban* — that let them starve their own parents while staying technically obedient to God. He's not weighing in on your household. He's exposing people who used religious technicality to abandon the people who raised them.

But the citation is real, and the weight behind it is real, and it's worth asking honestly: why does cursing a parent carry the same civil penalty as murder in the Torah? Because Israel wasn't just a religion. It was a covenant nation, and the family was the basic unit that transmitted the covenant itself — the story, the law, the memory of what God had done — from one generation to the next. To curse a parent wasn't just rudeness. It was an attack on the mechanism God had chosen to carry His promises forward. The nation had the authority and the obligation to protect that mechanism with civil law, the same way it protected against murder, because both were assaults on the covenant community's survival.

That civil penalty does not carry forward into the Church. The Church isn't a nation-state administering capital law; she's the Body of Christ, gathered from every nation, and no council or father has ever revived Exodus 21:17 as criminal code. If you've ever spoken to a parent in anger, you are not standing under that sentence. That much I want to be direct about, because I know how easily a wounded conscience turns a symbol into a verdict.

But — and this is the part I had to sit with — the *moral seriousness* doesn't evaporate just because the civil penalty does. Honor of parents is still one of the ten words. It's still quoted by Christ himself, without qualification, centuries after the nation that enforced it by law had already fallen. The weight moved from the courtroom into the conscience. It didn't disappear.

## What Chrysostom actually asks of the other side

Here's where I want to slow down, because this is the part that changed how I carried the whole thing. St. John Chrysostom, preaching on Ephesians in the fourth century, reads Paul's instruction to fathers — *"do not provoke your children to wrath, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord"* — and he does not read "provoke to wrath" as meaning *correction*. He reads it as contempt. As treating a child as property instead of as a free person with a soul. Disowning them, humiliating them, ruling them instead of forming them — that's provocation. Discipline itself is not the offense.

And then Chrysostom gives his positive vision, and it's the sentence I've carried since: *"Tempers are wanted, not talking; character, not cleverness; deeds, not words."* He's not describing behavior management. He's describing the slow forming of a soul — something closer to icon-writing than rule-enforcement. A parent's job, in his reading, isn't to produce compliance. It's to produce a person whose interior character has actually been shaped toward the good, by watching it lived, not just hearing it preached.

Then he reaches back to Eli, the priest from 1 Samuel, and this is the part I want you to sit with without letting it turn into guilt. Eli held the highest religious office in Israel. He instructed his sons in the law professionally, daily, by vocation. And his sons were wicked — because instruction from an office is not the same as formation from a character. Chrysostom's point isn't "you failed like Eli." It's narrower and harder: office and words, on their own, guarantee nothing. What forms a child's heart is the sustained, lived character of the parent standing in front of them, not the correctness of what's being said. That's not a verdict on you. It's a description of where the actual work happens — and it's worth knowing, however far along you already are in your own formation, because it tells you where to keep putting your attention.

And notice what Paul assumes when he tells children to obey — "in the Lord." Not bare compliance to authority as such, but obedience situated inside a living relationship with God. When a child is pulling away from faith itself, that phrase should tell you something: the goal was never behavioral submission. It was always a shared life inside the same relationship with God, and that can survive strain in a way that mere control never could.

## Where that leaves you

I don't have a clean ending for this, because I'm still walking it too. What I have is this: the death penalty was never meant for you, but the seriousness of honor was never meant to disappear either. And on the other side of it, Chrysostom isn't asking for a parent who never corrects — he's asking for one whose character, sustained over years, is the actual curriculum, with contempt nowhere in it. Formation is slower than control and it doesn't announce itself the way a rule does.

*"Fathers, do not provoke your children to wrath, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord."* That's the whole instruction. Not victory. Not resolution. Just the long, patient work of character over cleverness, deeds over words — walked out one ordinary day at a time, same as you're doing right now.
