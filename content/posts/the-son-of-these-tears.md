Part 1 assumed you get to start early — that you dedicate a child to God from infancy the way Hannah dedicated Samuel, and character follows the formation. This one is for the more common case, and the harder one: the child wasn't formed that way, or was, and still walked away. If that's where you are, I don't have a clean fix for you. What I have is a woman who lived through it in real time, and the shift in her that took years to arrive.

## Monica's two postures

Augustine tells the story himself, in Book III of the *Confessions*. By the time he reaches Carthage as a young man, his life is exactly what you'd fear for a child who slips the leash: he falls in with what he calls "a cauldron of unholy loves," and not long after, he falls in with the Manicheans — a sect he later describes as "men proudly raving, very carnal, and voluble," peddling a counterfeit truth that left him hungrier than before. This isn't a child who drifted a little. This is a full defection, doctrinal and moral both, and it didn't happen quietly. His mother watched it happen.

What Augustine records next is not a triumphant rescue. It's a change in his mother's own posture, and it happens in two stages.

First, she tries direct intervention. She goes to a bishop — a man Augustine describes as "reared in Your Church and well versed in Your books" — and asks him to sit down with her son, argue him out of his errors, teach him what's true. The bishop refuses. Not out of indifference: he tells her plainly that Augustine is "still unteachable, being inflated with the novelty of that heresy," too pleased with his own arguments to be reasoned out of them yet. His counsel to her is not to push harder. It's to wait. "Leave him alone for a time," he says, "only pray God for him; he will of himself, by reading, discover what that error is."

She doesn't accept this easily. Augustine records that she wept and pressed the bishop again, "shedding copious tears," asking him a second time to intervene directly. And here is the line that the whole passage turns on — the bishop, worn down but not annoyed in the way that dismisses her, finally answers: *"Go your way, and God bless you, for it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish."*

That sentence is not a formula and not a guarantee in the sense we'd want one — a timeline, a mechanism, a promise you could hold up in an argument. It's something closer to a redirection. He doesn't tell her the son of her arguments will be saved, or the son of her rules, or even the son of her example. He names the son of her *tears* — of the years of grief carried to God directly, after the direct approach had already failed. Augustine records that his mother received that sentence "as though it were a voice from heaven," and carried it for close to a decade before his actual conversion arrived. Nine years. She didn't get to see the fruit for nine years.

And near the end of Book III, Augustine adds something you don't get to hear from most children who drift: his own retrospective admission that God had been reaching him through her the whole time — that even in the years he rejected everything she believed, "Your answer through my watchful mother" was already at work in him, before he had any capacity to recognize it as such. The years of apparent silence were not actually silent. He simply couldn't hear it yet.

## The father who doesn't chase

That shift — from pressing to praying, from arguing to watching — is the same posture Christ describes in the parable of the prodigal son. The father in Luke 15 does not go to the far country after his son. He doesn't send messengers to negotiate, doesn't cut off the inheritance early to force a return, doesn't show up at the pig farm with an ultimatum. He waits at the point of return. And when the son is still "a great way off," the text says the father sees him and runs — the one undignified, uncalculated act in the whole parable — to close the distance the moment there's a distance to close.

The father's love was not less real during the son's absence. It just wasn't expressed as pursuit. It was expressed as watching, and as being ready to run the instant there was reason to.

## Grace already present

There's a smaller, less dramatic verse worth holding alongside this, because it protects you from despair in the meantime: 1 Corinthians 7:14, where Paul says a believing spouse's presence sanctifies an unbelieving one, and makes the children of that household holy rather than unclean. Paul isn't describing a finished conversion in either the spouse or the children. He's describing something more modest and, honestly, more useful to you right now: that a believer's presence genuinely touches a household with grace, even where nothing visible has landed yet. Your formation, however incomplete it feels in hindsight, was not wasted. It was doing real work in a register you can't audit from the outside.

## The path already built

Here's where this actually lands, for the blog this is written for. Not everyone starts at Hannah's dedication. The Church has never assumed they would. From the earliest centuries she has built a structured path specifically for people who arrive late, or come back late — the catechumenate. Cyril of Jerusalem, addressing candidates preparing for baptism, tells them plainly that they've "entered into a contest" and should "toil on through the race," because "another such opportunity you cannot have." He describes the work of formation like laying stone: "stone must follow stone by course, and corner match with corner... unless you fit them together in the one whole... the builder may build, but you will find the building unsound." That's a structure built for exactly the person who wasn't formed from infancy — who is starting the building later, stone by stone, in adulthood.

If your child left the faith, or never really entered it, that same structure is still standing, waiting for them the way it was waiting for Augustine. You don't get to build it for them. You get Monica's two options: press for a while, and then, when pressing stops working, wait — praying instead of arguing, watching instead of chasing, ready to run the moment there's a reason to.

Nine years is a long time to wait without proof it's working. I don't know how long yours will be. I know what the son of those tears turned out to become.
